## World building, terrain and level composition (Claude-of-Duty `src/world/`, MIT, commit d9b237b) — applied to Atomic Acres Gauntlet Test1/Test2/Nuke Town

**Where it helps us:** The single biggest lever for our thin, box-built maps is NOT more hand-written boxes — it is a write-through assembler plus a prop-prototype registry, which decouples "how many things are in the frame" from "how many draw calls the frame costs". Upstream builds a ~120x120 m street with eighteen buildings and several thousand props and lands at roughly 100 draw calls (src/world/builder.js:9-11, src/world/index.js:155-160, claude-of-duty). Our Test1 (52x38 m) and Test2 (64x48 m) are smaller than that street and currently spend one draw call per authored box, because only NON-solid, NON-shot boxes ever batch (src/additional-maps.ts:289-305, :300-305). Concretely, for us:

1. Test1/Test2 read thin because every prop is a box literal typed by hand in src/test-maps.ts. Upstream never types a prop position twice: props are prototypes (src/world/builder.js:178-211) and placement is a rule over occupancy predicates (src/world/dressing.js:66-83, :1731-1839). We already have the exact same predicate machinery for grass (src/grass-placement.ts, src/nuketown-lawn-field.ts:22-40) — generalising it from blades to props is the cheapest path from "small" to "dense".
2. The layout/generator split (src/world/layout.js is pure data; src/world/index.js:117-134 is a loop) is what makes a full-scale map editable. Our test-maps.ts mixes both, so scale means more typing, linearly.
3. Nuke Town gets the most from the facade bay programme + `solidSlabs` collision decomposition (src/world/buildings.js:283-318, src/world/util.js:591-618, src/world/kit.js:104-108): real window/door openings in the two houses whose colliders are derived from the same openings, which is precisely the shape our collider/visual parity gate demands in both directions (src/collider-visual-parity-gate.test.ts:17-25).
4. Draw-call safety comes from three stacked mechanisms, in this order of value for us: merge-by-material (biggest), instanced prototypes with per-instance variation (second), per-batch distance LOD (third). Chunking is fourth and barely matters at our map sizes.
5. Our hard constraint is unchanged and upstream actually violates it in one place: their scatter pass adds colliders inline (src/world/dressing.js:1809-1811). We take the placement pattern and reject that line — colliders stay authored in src/test-maps.ts / src/arena-layout.ts, art files stay presentation-only (src/test-maps-art.ts:7-9).

### Write-through Assembler: one merged mesh per material key — adopt: **yes**

No world module ever touches the scene graph. Every module writes into an Assembler object with four verbs: add(key, geo, matrix, opts) merges a transformed geometry into a per-material-key vertex accumulator; proto/place register instanced prototypes; box()/collideGeo() author collision separately; light() registers a punctual light. finalize() then walks the accumulators once and emits exactly one THREE.Mesh per material key (matrixAutoUpdate=false, userData.surface set, userData.collision=false because 'proxies own collision'), one InstancedMesh per prototype-chunk, and one invisible collision mesh per surface tag handed to physics. The accumulator (Accum) transforms positions and normals on the CPU as it appends, carries a vertex colour channel for wear/grime/AO masks, and picks Uint16 vs Uint32 indices by vertex count. A small geometry cache (cache/addOnce/releaseCache) keeps repeated kit pieces — a chamfered unit box, a window sill — from being rebuilt per bay, then frees them all after the level is built since the merged data is copied.

**Why:** This is the mechanism that makes every other density technique affordable. Without it, every prop we add to Test1/Test2 costs a draw call and the map stays small by economics.

**In our engine:** Extend the existing Builder in src/additional-maps.ts (type at :28-35) with a merge accumulator: `merged: Map<materialKey, {geometries: THREE.BufferGeometry[], material, castShadow, receiveShadow}>`. Then change `box()` (src/additional-maps.ts:90-152) so that after it pushes bounds into colliders/physicsColliders and the surface into shotSurfaces, the VISUAL mesh becomes a merge candidate for every box — not only the `!solid && !shots` ones it flags today at :121. Add `finalizeBuilder(builder)` that runs mergeGeometries per material key, adds one THREE.Mesh per key, and hides the source meshes. CRITICAL detail our repo already establishes: hiding a source mesh drops it from raycast authority, because src/legacy-main.ts:4502 filters `authority.raycastMeshes` to `mesh.visible || mesh.userData.collisionProxy === true`. So the finalizer must set `mesh.userData.collisionProxy = true` on every hidden source it merged, and a new test in src/test-maps.test.ts must assert that a hitscan against a merged wall still resolves to the named source mesh and its ballisticSurfaceId. Keep `batchPresentationOnlyBoxes` (src/additional-maps.ts:289) as-is for the art-only path in src/test-maps-art.ts; the new finalizer is the solid path.

**Cost:** ~150 lines in additional-maps.ts plus one parity test. Build-time only, no per-frame cost. The one real risk is the hidden-source/raycast interaction above — budget the test, not the merge.

**Upstream evidence:** `src/world/builder.js:41-80 (claude-of-duty) — Assembler fields: _mats/_static/_protos/_collide/_geoCache`, `src/world/builder.js:132-140 — add() merges into the Accum for a palette key`, `src/world/builder.js:152-171 — cache()/addOnce()/releaseCache()`, `src/world/builder.js:318-335 — finalize(): one Mesh per key, userData.collision=false`, `src/world/util.js:103-193 — Accum.add/build, CPU transform + vertex mask channel`, `src/world/index.js:106-140 — the whole level built through one Assembler, then finalize+releaseCache`, `src/world/index.js:155-160 — the stats line: static tris, instanced tris, instances, draw calls, collision tris`

### Layout as data, generator as a loop — adopt: **yes**

layout.js holds no code paths: STREET is five numbers, ALLEYS is a list of rects with a surface tag, BUILDINGS is an array of ~20-field specs (x/z/w/d/floors/wallKey/streetSide/damage/balconies/doorBays/roofProps and an optional rooms plan in NORMALISED room coordinates so a plan survives a change of footprint), GATE is a table of block extents, SET_PIECES is eight arrays of tuples (stalls, jerseys, sandbag walls, wrecks, palms, lamps, cables, laundry, hangings, rubble, tyres). index.js just iterates: registerProps, buildGround, `for (const spec of BUILDINGS) buildBuilding(...)`, buildGate, buildPerimeter, dressStreet, dressBuildings, scatterDebris, finalize. Background/infill masses are the same spec type with `skipSides` set so the unseen face is never generated.

**Why:** Full-scale composition means tripling the number of structures. That is only sane if adding one is adding a row.

**In our engine:** Split src/test-maps.ts (308 lines, currently data and construction interleaved — e.g. the tower at :77-104 and the sandbag/target loops at :107-114) into `src/test-maps-layout.ts` (frozen exported tables: TEST1_STRUCTURES, TEST1_LANES, TEST1_SET_PIECES, TEST2_*) and keep test-maps.ts as the loop. Precedent already exists in our repo and should be matched: src/arena-layout.ts is exactly this file for Nuke Town (ARENA_BOUNDS :24, CENTRAL_BUS :34-45, PARKED_VAN_LAYOUT :56-59, KERB_CAR_LAYOUT :70-75). Tests that today hardcode coordinates should import the table instead, which is what makes scaling the map a data edit rather than a test rewrite.

**Cost:** A mechanical refactor, one pass, no behaviour change. Guard it with the existing arena-layout.test / additional-maps.test snapshots so the built scene is byte-identical before and after.

### Bay-programme facade generation with hand-authored overrides — adopt: **yes**

A building is a footprint + floor count + a per-side facade programme, not a list of meshes. buildFacade divides each side into bays of ~3.05 m (`bays = round(len/3.05)`), and for each bay-per-floor rolls a kind from probabilities keyed off the spec: ground floor on a street-facing side gets door (if spec.doorBays names that bay) / shop (p 0.5 street, 0.25 secondary) / window (p 0.72); upper floors get window or arch (p 0.88 open face, 0.6 otherwise), upgraded to balconyDoor with p = spec.balconies. A ruined top floor rerolls to 'ragged'. Each kind pushes an opening rect into an array and a deferred deco closure. Crucially there is an escape hatch: `spec.bayKinds[side][floor][bay]` force-sets a kind (and options) for the bays that carry a sightline the map depends on — the shop the interior camera looks out of is authored by hand, not by dice. Window state is itself a distribution (boarded/open/shuttered/ajar/curtain/lit/glazed) biased by floor and damage, so no two openings on a wall are the same element.

**Why:** This is the actual answer to 'only a small portion of the map and style'. Density of ARCHITECTURE, not density of props, is what makes a map read full; and a bay programme gets you 200 authored openings from 20 rows of data.

**In our engine:** New file `src/test-maps-kit.ts` exporting `facade(builder, panel, program)` where `panel` is {origin, ry, length, height} and `program` is our own kind ladder (shed door / range window / container mouth / blank for Test1; french door / clerestory / balcony opening for Test2). Bay kinds emit BOTH an opening rect and a deco callback, exactly as upstream. The Test1 tower currently spends 12 hand-typed boxes on walls and parapets (src/test-maps.ts:77-92); a facade programme replaces that with one call per side and produces window openings for free. Keep the override table: our authored-shot baselines (src/authored-shot-resolution.test.ts, baselines/) depend on specific sightlines, so `bayKinds`-equivalent is not optional for us — it is what keeps the screenshot gates stable while the rest of the wall is generated.

**Cost:** ~300 lines for the kit plus per-map programmes. Needs the solidSlabs collision decomposition below to land in the same change, or it produces walls with visual holes and solid colliders — a direction-B parity finding.

**Upstream evidence:** `src/world/buildings.js:275-308 (claude-of-duty) — bay division and the per-kind probability ladder`, `src/world/buildings.js:310-318 — spec.bayKinds hand-override for load-bearing sightlines`, `src/world/buildings.js:320-423 — the kind switch: door/shop/window/arch/balconyDoor and the deferred deco closures`, `src/world/kit.js:122-134 — windowState(): per-opening variation biased by floor and damage`, `src/world/buildings.js:87-107 — floorSpec()/setback: upper floors pulled back to leave a roof terrace`

### solidSlabs: collision derived from the openings, not from the mesh — adopt: **yes**

facadeWall builds the visual panel with real punched holes (wallPanel with the openings list, bevelled, plus a few-millimetre fbm bow so nothing is perfectly flat). It then computes collision separately: solidSlabs(w, h, holes) splits the wall into vertical bands at every hole edge, and within each band into horizontal runs between the holes that overlap that band's midline, returning a list of solid rectangles. Each rectangle becomes one axis-aligned box proxy via slabBox(), placed by the same panel matrix. The result is that a doorway is a genuine hole in the collision hull, the BVH stays in the low thousands of triangles instead of chewing on every chamfer, and visual and collision can never disagree because both are functions of the same `openings` array.

**Why:** Without this, generated openings are the fastest way to manufacture parity findings. With it, openings are free and correct.

**In our engine:** Port solidSlabs verbatim in behaviour (our own code) into src/test-maps-kit.ts and have the facade emitter call `box()` once per solid rect with `solid: true, shots: true` while the punched visual panel is added as a merge-only mesh. Our Box2 collider model (src/additional-maps.ts:139-151) is already rectangle-based, so the mapping is exact — a wall with one door becomes 3 Box2s (left band, right band, header) instead of 1. This directly serves both directions of src/collider-visual-parity-gate.test.ts:17-25: direction A (every movement collider explained by a visible mesh) holds because each slab is under solid wall, and direction B (no visible mesh you can walk through) holds because the punched panel has no geometry where the collider is absent. Also register a per-slab BallisticSurface so the shot authority matches — our `box()` already does that when `shots` is true.

**Cost:** ~40 lines for solidSlabs plus wiring. Adds colliders (3 per opening instead of 1 per wall), which is fine: our physics is Box2 sweeps, not a triangle BVH.

**Upstream evidence:** `src/world/util.js:591-618 (claude-of-duty) — solidSlabs: band split, then run split, returns solid rects`, `src/world/kit.js:74-109 — facadeWall: punched visual panel, then `for (const s of solidSlabs(...)) A.slabBox(...)``, `src/world/builder.js:296-307 — slabBox(): a wall slab given in panel space, premultiplied by the panel matrix`, `src/world/builder.js:14-21 — the stated contract: 'Collision is authored separately from the visual mesh… so a doorway is a real hole in the collision hull'`, `src/world/builder.js:400-418 — collision root is a separate invisible Group, one mesh per surface tag, handed to physics.addStatic`

### Instanced prop prototypes with per-instance jitter and mask variation — adopt: **yes**

props.js/dressing.js declare prototypes: proto(id, {geo, key, tilt, sink, skirt, castShadow, chunk, maxDist, noPrepass}). Placement is `put(id, x, y, z, ry, s, masks, rx, rz)` which appends a matrix and an optional [wear, grime, ao] triple. When a jitter rig is armed for a pass, every prototype declared 'loose' (tilt > 0) gets ±12° of yaw, ±the prototype's own tilt on both horizontal axes, ±8% scale, and is sunk by its declared `sink` so a raised corner does not float — because 'the identical-clone read is the loudest tell in an instanced prop cloud'. The jitter draws from its OWN fixed-seed stream (Rng(0x9e3779b1)), deliberately not the placement stream, so tuning jitter cannot reshuffle every position in the level. At finalize, masks become an instanceColor attribute only if any instance in the batch declared one, and it multiplies the geometry's baked wear/grime/AO so no two crates weather alike. `put()` also drops a non-rotated dust fillet under any prototype with a `skirt` radius, unless the caller disarms `A.skirts` (a crate stacked on another crate is not standing on the ground).

**Why:** This is the difference between 'a few hundred props' and 'a few hundred draw calls'. It also directly buys the 'nothing perfectly repeated' quality bar our art gates look for.

**In our engine:** src/test-maps-art.ts already builds three InstancedMeshes by hand (tufts :611, cypress :709, shrubs :734). Generalise that into a `PropCloud` in the same file: `proto(id, geometry, material, {tilt, sink, maxDist})`, `put(id, x, y, z, ry, s, tint)`, `flush(root)`. Feed jitter from the existing `mulberry32` (src/test-maps-art.ts:19-27) but from a SEPARATE seeded stream from placement, per upstream's reason — our determinism contract (same file, :7-9) makes an accidental reshuffle a cross-peer divergence, not just a visual churn. Per-instance variation should use instanceColor multiplied into the canvas-painted map, which our texturedStandard path (:399) can carry with a one-line `vertexColors`-equivalent. All of this stays inside test-maps-art.ts, which is presentation-only by contract, so nothing here can add a collider.

**Cost:** ~120 lines. Runtime cost is one draw call per prototype-chunk regardless of instance count.

**Upstream evidence:** `src/world/builder.js:178-211 (claude-of-duty) — proto() and the tilt/sink/skirt/maxDist/chunk fields`, `src/world/builder.js:239-262 — put(): jitter application and the dust-skirt fillet`, `src/world/builder.js:360-382 — instanceColor built only if needed; per-instance masks`, `src/world/dressing.js:314-327 — jitterRig() on its own fixed-seed stream, and why`, `src/world/props.js:917-992 — the prototype table: skirt radii, LOOSE(tilt, sink), maxDist per prop class`

### Occupancy predicates drive the scatter — density from rules, not from typing — adopt: **yes**

Three cheap analytic predicates make placement automatic. inBuilding(x,z,m) tests the BUILDINGS footprints; isOpen(x,z,m) is 'not in a building AND (inside the street box OR inside an alley rect)'; groundY(x,z) returns the analytic floor height (road camber crown, kerb height on the pavement, 0.03 elsewhere); nearestWall(x,z) returns distance and outward normal. scatterDebris then places ~800 props in four loops, each a rule rather than a list: 340 along the building line with an EXPONENTIAL falloff away from the wall (`off = 0.12 + |gauss()| * 0.75`) because that is where wind and people put things; 180 on the road pushed to the gutters; per-alley counts proportional to AREA (`n = round(area * 0.85)`) with a wall-bias rejection test (accept p=1 within 1.2 m of a wall, p=0.45 otherwise); 220 weeds split between the kerb line and the wall bases. Every loop `continue`s if isOpen fails, so the rules can be sloppy and the result is still clean. A prop-kind distribution (a probability ladder over litter/brick/rock/weeds/can/plank/cinder/crate) picks the id.

**Why:** Ten rule loops replace two thousand typed box literals, and the density scales with the map instead of with the author's patience.

**In our engine:** We already have this exact machinery, for grass only: GRASS_GROUND_REGIONS + grassPlacementAllowed (src/grass-placement.ts) with a keep-out table in src/nuketown-lawn-field.ts:22-40 whose containment test verifies it against the REAL constructed arena's colliders. Promote it to `src/rendering/scatter-regions.ts` with a generic `ScatterRegion { bounds, surfaceKind }` + `keepOuts` + `allowed(x,z,margin)` + `groundY(x,z)`, and — this is the improvement over both upstream and our current code — DERIVE the keep-outs from `builder.physicsColliders` after the structural pass instead of maintaining a hand table. Upstream re-derives from the BUILDINGS array; we can do better because our colliders are already Box2s in the builder. Then Test1's hardpan scatter, Test2's terrace scatter and Nuke Town's yard prop scatter all become four rule loops each in test-maps-art.ts / nuketown art files. Keep the existing seeded-RNG determinism and the presentation-only rule; the scatter must never call `box()`.

**Cost:** ~200 lines to generalise plus the derived-keepout test. The derived keep-out removes a whole class of drift the lawn-field comment already worries about.

**Upstream evidence:** `src/world/dressing.js:51-83 (claude-of-duty) — inBuilding / isOpen / groundY`, `src/world/dressing.js:128-150 — nearestWall(): distance and outward normal`, `src/world/dressing.js:1736-1760 — the wall-line loop: gaussian falloff, isOpen reject, kind ladder`, `src/world/dressing.js:1779-1812 — alleys: area-proportional count, wall-bias rejection`, `src/world/dressing.js:1821-1839 — kerb/wall-base vegetation`, `src/world/index.js:428-432 — isOpen re-exported on the world API for other subsystems`

### Construction returns anchors; the dressing pass consumes them — adopt: **yes**

buildBuilding returns an `info` object accumulated during construction: floorY[], doors[{side,x,pm,worldPoint}], balconies[{side,x,y,w,d,pm}], windows[{side,f,x,y,w,h,pm,state}], awnings[], terraces[], roofSpec, roofY. Dressing then hangs props off those anchors rather than off coordinates: AC units bracketed beside upper-floor windows (p 0.34) with a condensate grime streak below, conduit boxes (p 0.16), washing lines strung across balcony windows (p 0.18), 1-4 props on every balcony plus a rug over the railing (p 0.55), signage over shop awnings and hanging signs over doors (p 0.5), doorstep junk in a ±1.3 m box around every door mouth, and roof clutter scaled by `spec.roofProps` placed on the ROOF plate (roofSpec) rather than the ground footprint — a bug the comment records, since using the footprint hangs water tanks over the terrace void. Everything is expressed in panel space via worldOf(pm, x, y, z) and ryOf(pm), so a prop placed 'beside this window' is correct on all four sides of every building for free.

**Why:** Correlated density is what separates 'a place' from 'noise'. Random scatter at uniform density still reads empty.

**In our engine:** Change buildTest1/buildTest2 in src/test-maps.ts to return an anchor bundle alongside the ArenaMap (`{ doorways, windowSills, deckEdges, containerTops, poolCoping, terraceEdges }`), and change applyTest1Dressing/applyTest2Dressing (src/test-maps-art.ts:516, :646) to take it as an argument instead of re-deriving positions from constants. Today the art file hardcodes its own copies of layout numbers, which is how art drifts from geometry. Anchors also give the dressing a legitimate reason to be dense in the RIGHT places — the tower deck edge, the container mouths, the pool coping — which is what makes a map read as inhabited rather than sprinkled.

**Cost:** Small; mostly a signature change plus threading. Pays for itself the first time a structure moves.

**Upstream evidence:** `src/world/buildings.js:158-167 (claude-of-duty) — the info accumulator declared`, `src/world/buildings.js:331,351,377,420 — anchors pushed as each bay kind is emitted`, `src/world/dressing.js:1417-1476 — window anchors -> AC units, condensate streaks, conduit, washing lines`, `src/world/dressing.js:1479-1549 — balcony and door anchors -> clutter, rugs, signage, doorstep junk`, `src/world/dressing.js:1551-1560 — roof clutter keyed to info.roofSpec, not the footprint`, `src/world/kit.js:1099-1111 — worldOf()/ryOf(): panel-space to world without per-call allocation`

### Per-batch distance LOD and spatial chunking — adopt: **partial**

Two independent cheap mechanisms. (a) Chunking: at finalize, any prototype with more than 24 instances and `chunk !== false` is split into spatial buckets by floor(x/CHUNK), floor(z/CHUNK), one InstancedMesh per bucket, so frustum culling has something to bite on. CHUNK is 64 m, deliberately coarse, with the reasoning stated inline: finer chunking culls a little better but multiplies draw calls through the prepass and four shadow cascades, which is the wrong trade at this map size. Props that must never disappear as a group (lamp posts, wrecks, palms) set `chunk: false`. (b) Distance LOD: prototypes declare `maxDist`; at finalize those batches go into a lodGroups list, and updateLod() runs one bounding-sphere distance test per BATCH per frame — `im.visible = (dist - radius) < maxDist` — with no per-frame allocation. The maxDist values are tiered by how small the prop is: dust skirts 42 m, litter 45 m, cans 45 m, weeds 40 m, glass shards 40 m, bottles 55 m, conduit boxes 55 m, produce 60 m, small rocks 70 m, planks and larger rocks 90 m.

**Why:** LOD is what lets us be genuinely dense near the player without paying for it across the whole map. Chunking is a real technique that our map sizes make a no-op — say so rather than cargo-cult it.

**In our engine:** Adopt the LOD half fully: give our PropCloud prototypes a `maxDist`, keep the batches in an array on the ArenaMap, and drive a `updateArenaPropLod(camera)` from the arena update tick — it is one Vector3.distanceTo per batch per frame. Tier it the way upstream does (smallest props culled soonest) and expose the tiers to src/adaptive-quality.ts so the low preset can scale every maxDist by a factor. SKIP the chunking half at our sizes: CHUNK=64 over Test1 (52x38) or Test2 (64x48) yields one bucket, so it is pure code with no effect; only Nuke Town (74x60, src/arena-layout.ts:24) could use two buckets, which is not worth the shadow-pass multiplication upstream itself warns about. If we later build a genuinely large map, revisit with a chunk around 20-24 m. Also reuse our existing `rustworksDetail: 'core' | 'performance' | 'quality'` tier (src/additional-maps.ts:107) as the orthogonal axis — LOD is distance, detail tier is preset.

**Cost:** LOD: ~30 lines plus a per-frame loop over a handful of batches. Chunking: skip.

**Upstream evidence:** `src/world/builder.js:33-39 (claude-of-duty) — CHUNK = 64 and the reasoning for coarse chunking`, `src/world/builder.js:344-357 — bucketing by floor(x/CHUNK), floor(z/CHUNK), only above 24 instances`, `src/world/builder.js:391-394 — lodGroups registration from proto.maxDist`, `src/world/builder.js:428-438 — updateLod(): one bounding-sphere test per batch, preallocated scratch`, `src/world/index.js:311-314 — updateLod called once per frame from WorldSystem.update`, `src/world/props.js:961-992 — the maxDist tier table by prop size`

### Terrain: gameplay-flat by construction, visually undulating everywhere else; collision is boxes, not the visual triangles — adopt: **partial**

The terrain is one 168 m PlaneGeometry at 42x42 segments. Height is `inStreet ? 0 : fbm3(...)` — the undulation is switched OFF inside the playable street box by a predicate on the vertex position, so the visual horizon is not a ruler-straight line where it meets the buildings but the ground the player walks on is exactly flat. The road is a separate subdivided strip carrying analytic camber `(1-(x/HW)^2)*0.055`, fbm wear, and a wheel-rut term, so it catches grazing sunlight — and its COLLISION is a single flat box (`A.box('dirt', 0, -0.2, ..., 0.42, roadLen)`), not the rutted triangles. Pavements are individual slabs of randomised length with gaps, a separately-scaled kerb stone, and driveway ramps cut where an alley mouth meets the pavement (detected by testing the ALLEYS rects against the running z). Where two ground materials meet, a `seam()` routine scatters irregular patches of BOTH materials straddling the line at ~1.15 m spacing plus loose stones across it, on its own fixed-seed RNG so the seam scatter cannot shift the rest of the level's draw sequence. The world exposes groundHeight() as a cheap analytic hint while stating that physics owns the exact answer.

**Why:** Flat-shaded box ground with ruler-straight material joins is the specific reason our maps 'read thin'. Fixing it costs nothing in gameplay authority because the visual layer never gets a collider.

**In our engine:** Keep our ground exactly as it is for AUTHORITY: `ground()` in src/test-maps.ts:41-45 makes a solid slab whose top face is y=0 so movement, ballistics and the viewmodel clamp all agree — that is stricter and better than upstream and must not change. Add a purely visual terrain layer on top: a subdivided plane at y≈0.02 with `solid: false, shots: false` whose displacement is forced to zero inside the playable rect (TEST1_BOUNDS / TEST2_BOUNDS, src/test-maps.ts:26-27) and rises only outside it, plus material-seam patch scatter along the hardpan/gravel and lawn/asphalt boundaries. For Nuke Town the same trick applies at the street/verge line (|z| = 8.8, already derived in src/nuketown-lawn-field.ts:12-17). The seam scatter must run on its own seeded stream, per upstream's reasoning, so tuning it never reshuffles prop placement across peers. Every one of these meshes is presentation-only and belongs in the art files, never in test-maps.ts.

**Cost:** ~150 lines. One extra merged mesh per material pair. Zero collision impact by construction.

### Never toggle light.visible to cull; drive intensity to zero — adopt: **partial**

### GPU texture forge: one surface program, N fullscreen draws, render targets ARE the textures — adopt: **yes**

There is no image data anywhere in the project (ARCHITECTURE.md:6 states no external art assets; the only 2D canvas usage in the whole repo is the minimap UI). Instead every surface is a single GLSL function with a fixed signature — `void owSurface(vec2 uv, out vec3 alb, out float h, out float rough, out float metal, out float ao)` — which is concatenated with a shared header, the noise library, rust helpers and a footer into one ShaderMaterial. The footer switches on a `uOutput` int uniform and writes a different packing per pass. A full 1K set is one framebuffer bind and four full-screen evaluations of the noise stack. Programs are cached per surface key so a second bake of the same surface at a different seed/tint recompiles nothing.

**Why:** Removes 76 MB of shipped PNGs (116 files in public/assets/original/textures) from the critical path for anything we convert, makes seed/tint variants free, and gives every surface a real height field, which is the prerequisite for normals, AO, parallax and cavity grime. **In our engine:** New file `C:\Users\david\projects\atomic-acres-gauntlet\src\rendering\texture-forge.ts`. r185's `three/webgpu` exports exactly the primitives: `rtt(node, width, height, options)` (three.webgpu.js:38447) wraps a node in a QuadMesh render-to-texture, and `QuadMesh` (three.webgpu.js:38115) is available directly if we want manual control of the render target. ### Sobel height-to-normal with physically scaled slope — adopt: **yes**

The surface program is evaluated once into a scratch half-float target with `uOutput == 0`, writing only the height. A second pass runs a 3x3 Sobel over that target: the horizontal and vertical kernel responses are divided by 8 to normalise the kernel weight, then divided by the texel size to convert a per-texel delta into a slope across the whole tile, then scaled by `uStrength = relief / worldSize` — the peak-to-trough depth in metres over the metres the tile spans. The result is a physically consistent tangent normal: a 5 mm mortar recess on a 1.35 m brick tile produces exactly the slope 5 mm over 1.35 m implies. Half-float is used for the scratch because an 8-bit height field makes the Sobel stair-step (generator.js:180-184), with an 8-bit fallback if the context cannot render half-float.

**Why:** Our two newest maps have no normal maps at all. This is the single change that most raises perceived surface quality per hour spent.

**In our engine:** This is the highest-value/lowest-risk item and it does NOT require the full forge — it can land against our existing CPU route first. `C:\Users\david\projects\atomic-acres-gauntlet\src\farcrysis-ground-materials.ts:196-212` already does a central-difference version of this (it keeps a Float32Array height field and derives an RGBA8 normal), so the pattern is proven in our codebase; port that function into a shared helper and give it upstream's two upgrades: a full 3x3 Sobel instead of the 2-tap central difference (less axis-aligned aliasing on diagonal features), and slope expressed as `reliefMetres / tileMetres` instead of our current unitless `relief` constants (farcrysis-ground-materials.ts:150-153). ### Periodic-by-construction noise library (seamless tiling with no seam-fixing pass) — adopt: **yes**

Every noise function takes a `per` (period in lattice cells) alongside the point, and wraps its hash lattice with `mod(i, per)`. A texture generated over uv in [0,1) with `p = uv * per` therefore tiles seamlessly with no mirroring, no offset-blend, no seam repair. fbm doubles both frequency AND period per octave so the whole stack stays seamless; domain warp, Worley, Voronoi-edge and the crack network all thread `per` through. Hashes are sin-free Hoskins-style integer mixes because sin-based hashes band badly on Apple GPUs at high lattice coordinates. Seeding is done by SHIFTING the argument (`p = uv * P + uSeed * 13.7`), which is noted as safe precisely because shifting the argument of a periodic function keeps it periodic. The library also includes a tileability-preserving anisotropic shear (`owShear` requires integer k/stretch so the lattice still wraps) used for brushed metal, mill scale and scratches.

**Why:** Seamlessness for free is what lets one 512 or 1K tile cover a 64 m ground plane. Our canvas painters (test-maps-art.ts:67-80 fbmMottle draws radial gradients at random positions) do NOT wrap — blobs near an edge are clipped, so every Test1/Test2 tile has a visible seam grid at the repeat counts we use (hardpan is [7, 5.4]).

**In our engine:** IMPORTANT CAVEAT for our engine: r185's built-in TSL noise nodes — `mx_fractal_noise_float`, `mx_worley_noise_float`, `mx_cell_noise_float` — are MaterialX ports and are NOT periodic. They will seam at the tile boundary. So we write our own periodic layer as TSL `Fn`s in a new `src/rendering/tsl-noise.ts`: `owHash12`, periodic gradient noise, `fbm(p, per, oct, gain)`, `worley(p, per, jitter)`, `voronoiEdge`, `cracks`, `warp`, `shear`, `scratches` — a direct conceptual port of noise.js with attribution to Claude-of-Duty (MIT) in the file header. Use the `mx_*` nodes ONLY for non-tiling, world-space runtime layers (macro variation sampled by world position never wraps, so periodicity does not matter there). Our existing `noise2D` in `src/farcrysis-ground-materials.ts:45-60` already gets this right on the CPU with its `wrap(value)` helper — same idea, ported to the GPU. This module is a prerequisite for the forge and should land first with unit tests asserting that `f(0,y) === f(per,y)`.

**Cost:** Medium: ~300 lines of TSL plus tileability unit tests. One-time.

### Nyquist discipline — every noise band is budgeted in texels, and a band that cannot be resolved is moved to another layer — adopt: **yes**

This is the most disciplined idea in the subsystem and it is enforced by comment-and-constant throughout. The detail bake reasons explicitly: 1024 px across 0.25 m means 0.244 mm per texel, `p = uv * 8` means a term at `p * K` puts 8K cells across 1024 texels i.e. 128/K texels per cell, so anything past K≈24 is under five texels and bakes as white noise — salt-and-pepper at mip 0, flat grey by mip 1. Every band is therefore capped at K = 20 (6.4 texels, 1.6 mm) and given real amplitude instead. Concrete refuses to author its 0.5-2 mm tooth at all, on the grounds that at 2.5 m over 1024 one texel is 2.4 mm, and delegates that band to the shared detail map which is tiled ten times finer. Gravel was moved from 512 to 1024 because at 512 its 9 mm grade was 2.5 texels wide; the fix note records that the three grades are now 34/19/9 mm (5.9 texels at worst) and that stones are separated from the bed by RELIEF and ROUGHNESS rather than by albedo value, because a 2.5:1 albedo step at a 10 mm period across a whole road is the textbook recipe for salt-and-pepper. Brick's face grain was re-authored from 2.2 texels per cycle to 7 for the same reason.

**Why:** It is the difference between a texture that looks detailed in a contact sheet and one that still reads at 15 m in game. It also directly explains why our textures probably look worse in motion than they do in `docs/assets/texture-contact-sheet.jpg`.

**In our engine:** This costs nothing to adopt and it is a rule, not code. Add to `C:\Users\david\projects\atomic-acres-gauntlet\AGENTS.md` (or the atomic-acres-procedural-art-authoring skill) a mandatory header comment on every texture painter stating: tile size in px, the world metres it spans at its authored repeat, the resulting mm/texel, and the finest feature it authors in texels. Then add a cheap automated check to the art tests: for each material, compute `metresPerTexel = tileMetres / sizePx` and fail if any declared feature size is under ~5 texels. Our current painters have no such budget — `paintCorrugated` at 512 with a [2.2, 1] repeat, `paintSandbag` at 512, `paintTravertine` at 1024 (test-maps-art.ts:429-434, 466-470) all pick a size with no stated world scale, which is exactly how a 'rich' texture mips to flat grey by 8 m. ### Two shared maps for the whole game: one micro detail tile and one 4-band macro variation tile — adopt: **yes**

Exactly two textures are built once at boot and bound into EVERY material: a 1K detail set spanning 0.25 m (micro albedo variation in rgb, micro height in alpha, plus its own Sobel normal) and a 256 px macro map whose four channels are four bands of low-frequency variation — R very-low fbm, G warped blotches, B mid fbm, A fine fbm. At runtime the detail layer is sampled at a much finer tiling than the base, its normal is added to the base normal in tangent space, its height drives an albedo speckle AND darkens the cavity/AO term (so aggregate reads as depth, not as a wash), and the whole thing fades out over `detail[3]` metres so the near ground gains detail instead of shimmering at range. The macro map is sampled twice at different scales in WORLD space, contrast-expanded (because averaging two fbm bands collapses toward 0.5 and turns the anti-tiling multiply into a 5% wash), and multiplied into albedo, plus an optional second much larger band at 8-16 m that is the signal surviving at 40 m when everything finer has mipped away. Crucially both shared maps skip the outputs nobody samples — the detail set has no ORM, the macro has neither ORM nor normal — saving a 1K RGBA8 mip chain and two 256 ones.

**Why:** One micro map fixes 'plastic at 0.5 m' on every material at once; one macro map fixes 'obvious tiling and flat colour at 20 m' on every material at once. Per-material cost is two texture fetches, not two textures.

**In our engine:** Two textures for the entire game — this is the cheapest big win after normals. Build both in the forge (or on the CPU first, reusing the noise helper) and expose them from a small singleton, e.g. `sharedSurfaceMaps()` in `src/rendering/texture-forge.ts`. On `MeshStandardNodeMaterial` the wiring is a `normalNode` that adds the detail tangent normal to the base normal scaled by a distance fade, and a `colorNode` that multiplies base albedo by the macro band. Distance fade comes from `positionView.length()` in TSL. Watch the colour-space trap upstream documents at generator.js:353-358: the detail map is DATA, not colour — stored sRGB-encoded, a 0.5 read back as 0.21 linear and the shader's `d - 0.5` term became a constant tint that could only darken. Both shared maps must be `NoColorSpace`. Our `farcrysis-ground-materials.ts:218` already passes `THREE.NoColorSpace` for its normal map, so the pattern exists; extend it. For Nuke Town this is what would stop the 12 m siding runs and the lawn from reading as one flat value at range.

**Cost:** Low-medium once the noise helper exists: ~2 surfaces' worth of authoring. Runtime: 2-3 extra fetches per pixel, fade-gated.

### detailWorld — the micro tooth is pinned to a fixed physical size, derived from the mapping scale — adopt: **yes**

A subtle but load-bearing fix. The detail layer's tiling was originally authored as 'tiles per base tile', which silently ties the micro layer's world scale to the macro layer's. A prop mapped at 0.55 m with detail[0] = 10 mapped the 0.25 m detail bake into 55 mm, so every 1.6 mm grain became 0.35 mm — under one pixel at 0.5 m — and the entire micro layer filtered away. The comment records the measurement that proved it: cranking the detail albedo amount from 0.42 to 2.5 on a market stall changed the frame by nothing at all. The fix derives the tiling from the mapping scale (`max(1.2, scale / detailWorld)` with detailWorld = 0.26 m, matching the bake's authored 0.25 m worldSize) unless the caller opts out, and skips the derivation for mesh-UV surfaces and anything mapped finer than 0.3 m — a viewmodel part mapped at 0.02-0.12 m wants detail an order of magnitude finer than a wall's, and forcing 0.26 m on it would put a 2 mm aggregate tooth on a bolt carrier.

**Why:** Without it the micro layer silently disappears on any prop or surface whose mapping scale differs from the one it was tuned at, and the failure is invisible — turning the knob up does nothing.

**In our engine:** Adopt as a rule in the same shared-map module: express detail tiling in metres, never as a repeat count, and derive the repeat from the surface's world footprint. Our `src/farcrysis-ground-materials.ts:31` already has the right instinct (`GROUND_TILE_METRES = 2`, with a comment that tile size is in metres 'so grain stays the same physical size across surfaces of very different extents') — generalise that constant into the shared surface layer so `test-maps-art.ts` stops using raw repeat pairs like `[7, 5.4]`, `[2.2, 1]`, `[1.6, 1]` (test-maps-art.ts:436-452) that encode no physical scale and cannot be reasoned about.

**Cost:** Trivial. One derivation, one opt-out flag.

**Upstream evidence:** `claude-of-duty/src/materials/shader.js:710-728`, `claude-of-duty/src/materials/shader.js:796-808`

### Stochastic two-scale de-tiling with a height-preserving blend — adopt: **partial**

Under `OW_DETILE` the same texture set is sampled a second time through a fixed rotation (cos/sin 0.803/0.596, ~36 degrees) and rescale (0.617) with a UV offset, and the two samples are blended by a low-frequency mask read from the macro map at world position. Derivatives are rotated identically so mip selection stays correct on the second sample. The blend is NOT a lerp: `owHeightBlend` weights each sample by `(1-t) + heightA * 0.6` versus `t + heightB * 0.6`, subtracts a common floor and renormalises, so the taller height wins locally instead of the two samples averaging into mush. Normals are blended by the same weights and renormalised. It is a per-surface opt-in (`detile` amount, 0 disables the extra fetches entirely), and it is disabled for triplanar surfaces where the three-axis blend already breaks the repeat.

**Why:** Height-preserving blending is the part most implementations get wrong — a plain lerp of two rotated samples produces a soft, low-contrast average that looks worse than the tiling it replaced.

**In our engine:** Worth it for exactly the surfaces where our repeat count is high and the tile is unmistakable: Test1 hardpan ([7, 5.4] repeats — the worst offender in the two new maps), the Nuke Town lawn and asphalt. Implement as an optional branch in the shared material factory, keyed off a per-surface `detile` number so the extra three fetches are opt-in, mirroring upstream's define gating. In TSL the gating is a build-time JS `if` around the node construction, which is strictly better than a `#define` — the node graph simply does not contain the second sample, and `NodeMaterial.customProgramCacheKey()` (see the note our own `src/farcrysis-tsl-foliage.ts:26` already makes about it hashing `*Node` properties) keeps the variants separate. Do NOT enable it on triplanar ground; do not enable it globally.

**Cost:** Medium effort, real runtime cost: triples albedo/ORM/normal fetches on the surfaces that enable it. Budget it per surface, not globally.

### World-space projection (planar dominant-axis and triplanar) — meshes need no UVs — adopt: **yes**

Three mapping modes chosen per surface: `mesh` (use vMapUv), `planar` (pick the dominant world axis of the normal and build a UV plus a tangent frame from it), and `triplanar` (build all three axis frames, weight by `pow(abs(n), 5)` normalised, and blend albedo, ORM and normal across them). Axis frames carry sign flips so back faces do not mirror. In planar mode the frame is re-anchored onto the true interpolated normal by Gram-Schmidt (`owOrthonormalise`). Triplanar samples the detail layer on the DOMINANT plane only — one extra fetch instead of three. A `localSpace` flag switches the whole projection into object space, with a precomputed projection-to-view matrix varying, so a prop keeps its texturing when it moves. Because the projection is world-space, texture density is uniform across an entire arena regardless of how the geometry was built, and modular kit pieces never show a UV seam.

**Why:** Uniform texel density across an arena with zero UV authoring, and it makes edge-wear masks and world-space weathering coherent across kit pieces that were modelled separately.

**In our engine:** r185 TSL ships `triplanarTexture` and `triplanarTextures` (confirmed exported from three/webgpu's TSL namespace), so the triplanar case is close to free — pass the albedo, ORM and normal texture nodes and a world-scale uniform. For the planar/dominant-axis case write a small `Fn` using `positionWorld` and `normalWorld`. Apply this first to ground and terrain-like surfaces (`src/nuketown-lawn-field.ts`, `src/nuketown-forest-surround.ts`, the Test1 hardpan plane) where our authored geometry has arbitrary UVs, and to any procedurally extruded cover in Test1/Test2. Keep the mesh-UV path for anything with authored UV layout. Two hazards worth pre-empting: triplanar costs 3x fetches so gate it by surface, and the normal blend must transform each axis normal into world space through that axis's own T/B/N before weighting (upstream shader.js:296-303) — blending raw tangent normals across axes is the classic wrong version and it produces lighting that swims as the camera moves.

**Cost:** Low for triplanar (built-in node), medium for the planar frame. Runtime: 3x fetches for triplanar surfaces — ground only.

**Upstream evidence:** `claude-of-duty/src/materials/shader.js:185-207`, `claude-of-duty/src/materials/shader.js:276-337`, `claude-of-duty/src/materials/shader.js:305-308`, `claude-of-duty/src/materials/shader.js:35-66`, `claude-of-duty/src/materials/shader.js:697-703`, `claude-of-duty/src/materials/library.js:144`, `claude-of-duty/src/materials/library.js:161`

### Macro-gradient normal tilt: 1-4 m relief the tile physically cannot carry — adopt: **yes**

A tile mapped at 2.5 m cannot contain a rut, a drift or a swale, because those features are larger than the tile. So on up-facing surfaces the shader finite-differences the macro map's B channel at a small UV offset, builds a 2D gradient scaled by a per-surface `macroRelief` amount, projects it into the tangent plane of the true world normal and adds it to the shading normal — then also darkens albedo by the same band. Stones and swales then catch the sun on one side and shadow on the other instead of reading as dither. The same trick is reused for cloth at a much finer scale to produce 8-14 cm drape folds on awnings, which the weave tile equally cannot carry.

**Why:** It buys the visual read of metre-scale ground relief with no geometry, no displacement, and no collision change — the only honest way to add that signal under our presentation-only rule.

**In our engine:** Ideal for the Nuke Town lawn and Test1 hardpan, where our ground is large, flat and tessellated too coarsely to carry metre-scale undulation as geometry. Add a `macroRelief` term to the shared material factory: sample the macro map's chosen channel three times (centre plus two small offsets), build the gradient, project it out of the world normal, add to `normalNode`. Gate it on `abs(normalWorld.y) > 0.62` exactly as upstream does so it never fires on walls. IMPORTANT for our contract: this tilts the SHADING normal only. It must not touch collision, the navigation mesh, or any shot surface — it is presentation-only by construction, which is exactly what our parity gates require, and worth stating in the module header so a later reviewer does not mistake it for displacement.

**Cost:** Low: three extra fetches on up-facing surfaces of enabled materials. Half a day.

### World-space contextual weathering: dust, runoff streaks, ground splash, and a dust wedge at the junction — adopt: **partial**

None of this is baked in the tile; it is evaluated per pixel from world position and world normal, so it is consistent across every mesh regardless of UVs. Four layers. (1) Up-face dust: weighted by `up*up`, breaks up against the macro map, raises roughness, kills metalness and partially flattens the shading normal toward the geometric normal because dust is loose powder that fills relief. (2) Rain runoff: `owRunoff` models real drips — sources sit in ~65 cm columns along the wall, only some columns have anything running (a per-column hash gate), each run fades in over the first 15 cm below its source and out over the next 1.5 m, with a bell falloff across the column so runs have soft sides instead of cell walls, and sources are spaced roughly one storey apart with per-column jitter. The comment is explicit that a 10:1 vertical stretch of value noise across a whole wall is a wood-grain generator, not weathering. Rust bleed is layered under a hashed subset of columns to fake metal fixings. (3) Ground splash: a hard dirt band in the bottom ~20 cm plus thinning spray above it, keyed off world Y minus a `groundY` uniform the material system pushes to every material. (4) The dust wedge: a 25-40 cm wedge of the ground's own dust piled against the wall base, with the explicit note that without it every wall/ground junction in the frame is a razor cut. Each layer moves albedo, roughness, metalness and AO together rather than tinting alone.

**Why:** Contact grounding is the highest-value part. A wedge and a splash band at every wall/ground junction changes the read of a whole arena far more than any tile detail does, and it costs almost nothing.

**In our engine:** Adopt the ground-splash band and the dust wedge first — they are the cheapest and they fix the most visible defect in Nuke Town and Test2: buildings, planters, pool coping and cover meeting the ground on a perfect line. Both need only `positionWorld.y`, a `groundY` uniform, and `normalWorld` verticality; roughly 20 lines of TSL in the shared material factory, plus a `setGroundLevel(y)` that walks live materials, mirroring index.js:259-266. Adopt the runoff column model second, and only for Test2 (stucco villa walls) and Nuke Town siding where it reads. Skip the rust-bleed sub-layer entirely — it is a Levantine-industrial signature that would look wrong on American suburbia. Everything here is presentation-only and touches no gameplay authority.

**Cost:** Wedge + splash: low effort, ~6 ALU-cheap operations per pixel. Full runoff model: medium effort, a few extra macro fetches on vertical faces.

### Curvature-baked vertex masks: convex edges wear, concave creases collect grime — adopt: **yes**

A CPU geometry pass writes a 3-channel `color` attribute — r = edge wear, g = grime, b = extra AO — all defaulting to 0, which the shader treats as no effect, so it is always safe to add to any geometry. It first CLUSTERS vertices by quantised position (round to 1/8192) because hard-edged kit geometry duplicates vertices per face, so raw adjacency never crosses an edge and every box would come out perfectly clean. Per cluster it accumulates the summed unit offsets to neighbours and the summed normal; `dot(avgNormal, avgOffset) < 0` means the surface folds away from the normal, i.e. convex, `> 0` means concave. A separate 'spread' term (how much the normals at a cluster disagree) separates 'on an edge' from 'merely tilted'. Up-facing vertices get extra wear, down-facing get extra grime. In the shader, wear is further broken up by macro noise and biased toward the HIGH points of the height field, with a deliberately shallow bias (0.55 to 1.0, not 0 to 1) because on a road whose height field IS its aggregate a full bias turns wear into a per-stone brightener and every stone crown lights up. The clustering was optimised from a string-keyed hash map to an integer hash with chained buckets after it was measured at 50-65 ms of the 110-118 ms bake over 202k vertices.

**Why:** It is the single cheapest way to stop authored modular geometry reading as clean extruded boxes, it is 100% CPU-side and presentation-only, and it needs no forge, no WebGPU and no texture work — it can land completely independently of everything else here.

**In our engine:** Port `bakeMasks` almost directly into `C:\Users\david\projects\atomic-acres-gauntlet\src\rendering\vertex-wear-masks.ts` — it is plain BufferGeometry maths with no renderer dependency, so it runs identically in vitest and headless, which suits our gate culture. Apply it to the authored kit geometry in the Test1/Test2 builders and the Nuke Town house/cover geometry. Consume it in the material factory: `vertexColors = true`, then in TSL read the colour attribute and use r to lerp albedo toward a wear colour and raise/lower roughness, g to lerp toward a grime colour, b to attenuate AO. Two traps upstream documents that we must honour: (a) upstream had to force wear metalness to 0 by default because a 0.5 default turned every worn edge on concrete, plaster, brick and timber half-metal (shader.js:756-765) — only metal surfaces may raise it; (b) our shared material factory must override three's `color_fragment` behaviour so vertex colours are read as MASKS and never multiplied into albedo (upstream replaces the chunk outright at shader.js:669). On a node material that is automatic — we simply never wire the vertex-colour attribute into `colorNode` directly.

**Cost:** Low effort (~200 lines, direct port). Boot cost measured upstream at 110-118 ms over 202k vertices; ours is smaller. Zero per-frame cost.

**Upstream evidence:** `claude-of-duty/src/materials/masks.js:1-14`, `claude-of-duty/src/materials/masks.js:72-115`, `claude-of-duty/src/materials/masks.js:117-186`, `claude-of-duty/src/materials/masks.js:188-215`, `claude-of-duty/src/materials/shader.js:573-596`, `claude-of-duty/src/materials/shader.js:16-20`

### Bounded texture memory: quality-scaled sizes, bake cache keys, skipped outputs, released scratch — adopt: **yes**

Five compounding mechanisms. (1) Every bake size is multiplied by a quality factor (low 0.5, medium 0.75, high 1.0), snapped to a 128 multiple and then to the nearest power of two so mip chains stay clean. (2) Texture SETS are cached on a key of name+size+seed+tints+params, and MATERIALS are cached separately on name plus a stable serialisation of the options, so identical (name, opts) return the identical instance and the meshes batch. (3) The two shared maps skip the outputs nobody samples — the detail set builds no ORM, the macro builds neither ORM nor normal — with the saving quantified in the comment as a 1K RGBA8 mip chain (5.6 MB), two 256 ones, and four full-screen noise evaluations at boot. (4) The half-float scratch height targets, which exist only to feed the Sobel, are pure intermediates but were the largest allocation the forge makes (~10.5 MB for the 1K/512/256 set); they are released once the bake burst has clearly finished, with `_heightRT()` recreating on demand so a late bake still produces a bit-identical texture. (5) `dispose()` tears down every material, set, program and render target.

**Why:** Procedural generation makes texture variants free to ASK for, which is exactly how VRAM budgets get blown. The cache key is the control.

**In our engine:** Adopt (1), (2), (3) and (5) directly in `src/rendering/texture-forge.ts`. Wire the quality factor to our existing `src/adaptive-quality.ts` and `src/pass65-settings.ts` graphics runtime so the low preset genuinely halves texture VRAM rather than only touching resolution. The set/material two-level cache matters more for us than for upstream because our arena builders are re-run on rebuild — and note our own recorded gotcha 'Module registry rebuild leak' (art registries doubling per arena rebuild): a keyed cache plus a real `dispose()` on arena teardown is the direct fix. Skip mechanism (4)'s five-second idle heuristic (see notWorthIt) and just dispose the scratch target synchronously at the end of the bake batch — we control the batch, upstream did not. Add a boot-time assertion logging total forge VRAM so the budget is visible in the gauntlet output.

**Cost:** Low. It is bookkeeping around the forge, written once.

### Metalness discipline: bare metal is 1, every oxide/paint/dirt layer on top of it is 0 — adopt: **yes**

Stated as the single most important physical rule in the metals file. `METAL_RUST` starts at metalness 1 for the steel, then blends metalness toward 0 through the rust mask (`mix(1.0, 0.0, smoothstep(0.15, 0.55, rust))`) and back toward 1 through the scratch mask where a scratch exposes bright metal — so the specular response tracks the actual material at each texel. ### Parallax occlusion mapping from the packed height, distance-faded, with explicit-gradient sampling — adopt: **no**

Under `OW_PARALLAX` the shader marches the height stored in albedo.alpha along the tangent-space view vector, up to 48 iterations with the layer count interpolated between a per-surface max and 8 based on the grazing angle, then does one linear interpolation between the last two steps to find the intersection. The whole effect is faded out between two distances (default 6-14 m). Critically, every texture read in the march uses `textureGrad` with derivatives computed once before the march, because implicit derivatives inside a divergent loop select the wrong mip; an `OW_NOGRAD` fallback exists. Per-surface depths are small and physical — brick's 0.024 m over a 0.12 height range is described as ~2.5 mm of mortar parallax.

**Why:** It is the most expensive item in upstream's shader by a wide margin, it breaks silhouettes (which matters more in our peeking sightlines than in their street scenes), and it buys the least once normals, micro detail and macro variation are in place — those three already deliver most of what parallax is reaching for.

**In our engine:** Do not port. If a specific surface genuinely needs depth at close range in Test2 or Nuke Town — brick coursing, a grating, corrugation — spend the polygons instead: our arenas are already geometry-heavy and a real bevel is cheaper to reason about, survives at silhouettes, and does not need a new distance-fade budget or a 48-iteration loop under WebGPU. If it is ever revisited, the one detail worth carrying over regardless is the explicit-gradient rule: any texture fetch inside a loop must use precomputed derivatives, or mip selection breaks.

**Cost:** Would be high: up to 48 dependent texture fetches per pixel on enabled surfaces, plus a fade budget to tune. Not justified.

### Assembler with prop prototypes, spatial chunk buckets and per-prototype cull distance — adopt: **yes**

Claude-of-Duty never touches the scene graph from art code. Every module writes into one `Assembler`: `proto(id, spec)` declares an instanced prototype (geometry + palette key + flags), `place/put/putS` push a Matrix4 and an optional 3-float mask into that prototype's arrays, and `finalize()` turns each prototype into InstancedMeshes. The clever part is finalize: if a prototype declared `chunk !== false` and has more than 24 instances, its matrices are bucketed by `floor(m.elements[12]/64) * 97 + floor(m.elements[14]/64)` — a 64 m spatial grid keyed off the translation column — and ONE InstancedMesh is emitted per occupied bucket. Each bucket mesh gets `computeBoundingSphere()` so three's frustum test sees real instance bounds. Prototypes that also declare `maxDist > 0` are pushed onto `lodGroups`; every frame `updateLod(camera)` does one bounding-sphere-vs-camera distance test per bucket and flips `im.visible`. Chunk size is explicitly reasoned, not arbitrary: the comment says finer chunking culls better but multiplies draw calls through the depth prepass and four shadow cascades, which is the wrong trade at 120 m. All prototypes sharing a palette key share ONE cached material (`mat(key)`), so all foliage — weeds, shrubs, palm fronds — is one pipeline however many buckets exist.

### Per-prototype 'looseness': jitter rig on a forked RNG stream — adopt: **yes**

A prototype declares `tilt` (radians of allowed out-of-true) and `sink` (how far to lower it so the raised corner does not float). During the set-dressing pass the assembler is armed with `A.jitter = { rng, yaw: 0.209, scale: 0.08 }`; `put()` then adds +/-12 deg of yaw, +/-tilt on both horizontal axes and +/-8% scale to every prototype whose tilt > 0, and lowers y by sink. The rig runs on its OWN fixed-seed RNG (`new Rng(0x9e3779b1)`), explicitly forked from the placement stream, so that changing jitter never shifts subsequent placement positions and re-rolls the whole layout. The stated reason for the jitter at all: identical clones all plumb and all the same size is the loudest tell in an instanced prop cloud.

**Why:** The forked-stream discipline is the transferable insight, not the numbers. Our grass field already consumes its RNG stream identically regardless of rejections for exactly this reason (instanced-grass-field.ts:440-444, 'a keep-out change never rearranges the rest of the field') — so the idea is already proven in our codebase and just needs to be the default for every scatter kind, not only grass. Our Test2 cypresses currently vary only by `tree % 5` and `tree % 3` (src/test-maps-art.ts:717-718): twelve cones in two dead-straight rows at 11 m pitch, all vertical, three distinct sizes. That is the clone read CoD is describing.

**In our engine:** Put `tilt`, `sink` and `scaleJitter` on the `proto()` spec in the new `src/rendering/scatter-assembler.ts`, and give the assembler an `armJitter(seed)` / `disarmJitter()` pair that uses a SEPARATE mulberry32 stream from the placement stream (we already have `mulberry32` in instanced-grass-field.ts:264 — export it or lift it into a shared `src/rendering/scatter-rng.ts`). ### Derived density: rejection-sample against occupancy, bias by distance to walls, pick from weighted tables — adopt: **yes**

Density is never a hand-placed list. `scatterDebris` runs several thousand candidate draws with a fixed count per zone, then filters and weights: candidates near the building line use an exponential falloff off the wall (`0.12 + |gauss()| * 0.75`), every candidate is rejected unless `isOpen(x, z, margin)` (not inside any building footprint, and inside the street or an alley rect), alley candidates additionally get a `wallBias` of 1.0 within 1.2 m of a wall and 0.45 beyond it, and ground height comes from a real `groundY(x,z)` that knows the road camber and the kerb step. What lands is chosen from a cumulative weight ladder (litter 30%, bricks 16%, rocks 12%, weeds 10%, ...) that differs per zone: the street ladder gives weeds 10%, the alley ladder gives weeds 10% plus shrubs 8%, and a dedicated 220-candidate vegetation pass runs only along the kerb line and wall bases at 78% weeds / 22% shrub. **Why:** We already do the primitive version of this — `placementAllowed(x, z)` in `src/rendering/instanced-grass-field.ts:134` is the keep-out filter, and Test1's scrub uses `nearEdge = |x| > 21 || |z| > 16` (src/test-maps-art.ts:622). What we lack is (a) graded density rather than binary allow/reject, and (b) a weighted species table, which is what makes a scatter read as an ecology instead of a stamp. CoD's camera keep-out list is also directly the shape of an Atomic Acres gameplay-clearance rule.

**In our engine:** Change `placementAllowed` from `(x,z) => boolean` to an optional `placementDensity: (x, z) => number` in [0,1] and keep the boolean as a shim (density > 0). Reject a candidate when `rng() > density(x,z)` — CoD's `wallBias` pattern exactly. Then add `src/rendering/scatter-species.ts` with `pickWeighted(entries, roll)` (a cumulative ladder like fable's SelectDetailObject) so one scatter pass can emit several prototypes. For Test1 write density as a function of distance to the fence line and to the firing lanes; for Test2 as distance to the terrace balustrade, hedge beds and pool coping. ### 32 m periodic Poisson tile with layered inter-layer clearance distances — adopt: **yes**

This is Fable's actual density authoring, faithfully reimplemented. Each vegetation generator has an ordered list of layers, and layer N is authored as a VECTOR of distances of length N+1: entries 0..N-1 are the minimum clearance this layer must keep from each PRIOR layer, and the last entry is its own self-spacing. Points are generated once into a 32 m periodic tile (`PeriodMetres = 32`) by dart-throwing with a toroidal-wrapping grid index (`PeriodicPointIndex.IsClear` wraps deltas via `min(d, 32-d)`, so the tile is seamlessly repeatable), targeting `32*32*0.55 / spacing^2` points with an attempt cap. The tile is then stamped across the map by `Tile()`. A coarser export floor can decimate a finer cached pattern in place (`ThinPeriodic`) without regenerating, so lowering density is a strict subset of the higher density — no reshuffle. Real numbers from the Fisher Creek spec: birch trees 4.5 m self-spacing; saplings 3 m from birches, 4 m from each other; bracken 1.5/1/1/1; grass blades 0.25 m; tall grass 0.12 m; fallen leaves 0.2 m.

**Why:** This solves the exact problem our jittered grid cannot: layer relationships. Our grass field is one candidate per grid cell (`instanced-grass-field.ts:438-448`) which is fine for a uniform lawn, but the moment a map has trees + saplings + bracken + grass on the same ground you need 'no sapling within 3 m of a birch' or the scatter grows a bush through every trunk. Nothing in our repo expresses that. The 32 m periodic tile is also a memory and determinism win — one point set, stamped, identical on every peer, and decimation for a lower quality tier is a subset rather than a re-roll (which matters because our peers must agree).

**In our engine:** New `src/rendering/detail-placement.ts`: `buildPeriodicTile({ periodM = 32, layers, seed })` where each layer is `{ name, spacings: number[], objects: WeightedObject[] }` and `spacings.length === layerIndex + 1` (enforce that with a throw, as LocalDetailPlacement.cs:56 does). Port `PeriodicPointIndex` — the toroidal wrap in `IsClear` is what makes tiles seamless and is easy to get wrong. Then `tileOver(pattern, originX, originZ, width, depth)` emitting `{x, z, hashX: round(worldX*256), hashZ: round(worldZ*256)}`. **Cost:** ~180 lines of TypeScript plus tests. The dart-throwing generation is O(points * neighbours) with the grid index — milliseconds. One day including the layer-clearance test.

### Position-hashed per-instance variation (hash the quantised world position, never the instance index) — adopt: **yes**

Every per-instance decision in Fable's detail placement is a hash of the point's QUANTISED WORLD POSITION plus a stream id, not of its index in the array. Points carry `HashX/HashY = round(world * 256)` (1/256 m grid), and `DetailHash(seed, x, y, stream)` is an xor-multiply-shift finaliser mixed with 0x9E3779B9/0x85EBCA6B/0xC2B2AE35 constants. Stream 2 picks which mesh from the weighted object list, stream 3 the scale variation in [-1,1], stream 4 the yaw in [0,tau). Because the input is the world position, the same point produces the same tree whether it was the 3rd or the 3000th accepted candidate, whether neighbouring candidates were rejected, and whether the map is tiled from a different origin.

**Why:** We have a live latent bug shaped exactly like this. `instanced-grass-field.ts:277` defines `instanceHash(salt)` as `fract(sin(instanceIndex * 12.9898 + salt*7.13) * 43758.5453)` and uses it for both wind phases. Today each region is one mesh so it is merely arbitrary; the moment we chunk a region for LOD (technique 1) instanceIndex restarts at 0 per bucket and the wind phase field becomes discontinuous at every chunk boundary — a visible seam across the lawn. Position hashing is immune to chunking, to rejection filtering, and to any future reordering, and it is what makes our seeded determinism robust rather than incidental.

**In our engine:** In `src/rendering/instanced-grass-field.ts`, replace `instanceHash(1)/instanceHash(2)` with a TSL hash of `positionWorld.xz` quantised to 1/256 m — the graph already reads `positionWorld` for the gust wave at line 285, so nothing new is plumbed. Mirror the same function on the CPU side in `src/rendering/detail-placement.ts` as `detailHash(seed, hx, hz, stream)` (port the xor-multiply-shift finaliser; it is 6 lines) and use it for yaw, scale variation and weighted species pick in the scatter assembler, replacing the `k % 5` / `k % 3` idioms in src/test-maps-art.ts:717 and the `tree % 7` idiom in the vegetation skill. Add a unit test asserting that placing the same field with a keep-out change does not alter the yaw/scale/species of any surviving instance — that is the invariant position hashing buys, and it is exactly the kind of thing our parity gates should own.

### Density derived from the ground surface itself, with a dominant-channel rule and a water veto — adopt: **yes**

Which vegetation grows where is not authored per-instance; it is read off the terrain's material paint. Each landscape material name maps to a `LOCAL_DETAIL_GENERATOR` entry, and a cell only activates a generator if its DOMINANT channel (Material0) names that material — Material1/2 are blend contributors and are explicitly forbidden from activating detail, with the reason spelled out: doing so 'plants trees across paths, water transitions, and no-detail paint'. A separate `primarilyWater` mask vetoes any cell whose dominant material is a water/sea pass. The generator spec then names its layers and their meshes, so 'GROUND_GRASS' means grass blades at 0.25 m and 'GROUND_BIGTREES' means large oaks at 5 m plus dry grass at 1.5 m. The terrain's own path channels get promoted to a single replacement surface rather than a coplanar overlay, deliberately trading a blend for avoiding Z-fighting.

**Why:** It makes ground-cover density a property of the SURFACE rather than a hand-tuned rectangle list, which is the difference between art you tweak in one place and art you tweak in twelve. Our current model is rectangles plus a boolean filter (`GrassRegionRect` at instanced-grass-field.ts:60). It also gives a principled place to hang the 'never grow on X' rules that our parity gates care about: paths, capture zones, pool decks, concrete.

**In our engine:** Introduce a `surfaceAt(x, z) -> SurfaceKind` provider per arena (Test1: 'range-dirt' | 'gravel' | 'concrete-pad' | 'fence-verge'; Test2: 'travertine' | 'lawn' | 'garden-bed' | 'gravel-path' | 'pool-coping'), sourced from the same authored geometry the arena builder already uses so it cannot drift. ### Distance LOD by LAYER SELECTION rather than by mesh swapping — adopt: **yes**

### Slope-aware placement: heightfield sample, per-layer surface offset, optional tilt-to-normal — adopt: **yes**

Every detail instance samples the terrain height bilinearly from the fine heightfield, adds the layer's authored `SurfaceOffset` (0 for trees, 0.01 m for saplings, 0.012-0.02 m for grass/leaves — i.e. small things sink slightly to hide the seam), and layers can declare `TiltToSlope`. When set, the up vector comes from a central-difference normal of the heightfield at +/-0.25 m and the instance basis is built by Gram-Schmidt: project the yaw forward vector off the up, cross for right, re-cross for a true orthonormal frame. Note which layers set it: grass blades, mushrooms, brambles and fallen leaves tilt to slope; TREES DO NOT — trunks stay vertical regardless of terrain.

**Why:** Our grass field takes a `groundY(x, z)` hook (instanced-grass-field.ts:132) and a `rootSinkM` (default 0.02 m — the same trick, independently arrived at) but has no notion of surface normal, so on any sloped ground every blade stands plumb and the field reads as a decal on a ramp. Test2 is explicitly a HILLSIDE estate and `src/farcrysis-mountains.ts` is all slope. The trees-stay-vertical rule is the non-obvious half and worth writing down.

**In our engine:** Add an optional `groundNormal?: (x, z) => THREE.Vector3` alongside `groundY` in the scatter assembler and in `InstancedGrassFieldOptions`, plus a per-prototype `tiltToSlope: boolean` (default false). Compute the basis exactly as LocalDetailTransform does — Gram-Schmidt off the yaw forward vector, not `Quaternion.setFromUnitVectors`, which loses the authored yaw. Default `tiltToSlope: true` for grass/ground cover/leaves and FALSE for cypresses, palms, poles and any trunk. Provide a default central-difference normal helper that wraps whatever `groundY` the caller passed, so no arena needs a second heightfield source.

### Directory-as-ownership with runtime-only cross-subsystem access — adopt: **partial**

Every subsystem is a class with `static id` and `static deps`, lives in exactly one directory, and is forbidden from importing any other subsystem's module. Reaching a peer is always `ctx.get('fx')` at call time, never a top-level import. The registry is the only thing that knows the mapping from id to instance, so the dependency graph is a declared list of strings rather than an emergent property of the import graph. A markdown table maps id → directory → what it owns, and shared files (`src/core/`, `src/main.js`, `tools/`) are explicitly owned by nobody but the lead. The stated reason is parallel agent work, but the regression-prevention effect is the real prize: you cannot accidentally reach into another system's internals, because you never have a reference to its module — only to its instance, through a surface you had to look up by name.

**Why:** The ownership table and the one-directory-per-system rule are exactly what we lack — legacy-main.ts has no owner boundaries at all. But the string-keyed `ctx.get(id)` half is a downgrade for us: it throws away every compile-time guarantee TypeScript gives, and our regressions are precisely the kind (a literal gate, a missing attachment, a mask flipped) that a type system catches for free.

**In our engine:** Create `src/systems/<name>/index.ts` per extracted system with an exported `SystemId` const and a `deps` array, plus a `docs/OWNERSHIP.md` table mirroring claude-of-duty/ARCHITECTURE.md:54-71 listing directory → owns → who may call it. Keep TypeScript imports for *types* (`import type { VisibilitySystem }`), but pass the *instance* through a typed context object rather than importing the module that constructs it. That gives us COD's isolation without COD's stringly-typed lookup. Enforce with an eslint `no-restricted-imports` rule: nothing under `src/systems/a/**` may import `src/systems/b/**` except its `types.ts`.

**Cost:** Half a day for the doc + lint rule; the real cost is the extraction itself, sized per system below.

**Upstream evidence:** `claude-of-duty/ARCHITECTURE.md:11-14`, `claude-of-duty/ARCHITECTURE.md:54-71`, `claude-of-duty/src/core/registry.js:14-15`, `claude-of-duty/src/core/registry.js:22-43`, `claude-of-duty/src/core/engine.js:47-62`

### Topologically resolved init order and a single frame-order authority — adopt: **yes**

`Registry.resolve()` does a depth-first topological sort over `static deps`, throwing on cycles (`dependency cycle at "x" (via y)`) and on a dep that was never registered. Registration order in main.js is therefore irrelevant. The Engine then owns the entire frame sequence in one ~35-line method — input.beginFrame, N fixedUpdate substeps with a spiral-of-death clamp, update, lateUpdate, render, input.endFrame — and caches per-method system lists so it never re-filters. No subsystem schedules anything. `registry.with('update')` is the only iteration point, so 'this ran before that' is a property of one file, not of call-site ordering scattered through a monolith.

**Why:** Ordering regressions are invisible until they aren't. A declared dep graph that throws on a cycle is the cheapest possible structural test, and it runs on every boot including in CI.

**In our engine:** Add `src/systems/registry.ts` with the same topo-sort + cycle throw, and a `registry.test.ts` that constructs the real system list and asserts `resolve()` succeeds and returns a specific order. ## Lighting, sky and atmosphere (Claude-of-Duty `src/sky/*` + `src/render/*`)

**Where it helps us:** Upstream's headline lesson is that its "lighting rig" is almost nothing: one directional sun, one moon, and NO AmbientLight or HemisphereLight anywhere in the world scene (grep across `src/` finds hemisphere lights only in the four standalone preview harnesses and on the viewmodel scene, `src/render/index.js:274`). Everything that reads as ambient is either the PMREM baked from the live sky, or two normal-gated irradiance bands injected into `lights_fragment_maps`. Our arenas do the opposite: a flat `AmbientLight` at `ambientIntensity` (`src/legacy-main.ts:3227`) plus a PMREM of an 8-bit sRGB canvas/WebP sky (`src/rendering/arena-environment-ibl.ts:72-73`), which is exactly the "uniform lighting" upstream calls out as the WebGL-demo tell. Four changes move Test1/Test2 and Nuke Town the most, in order of payoff-per-risk: (1) replace flat `AmbientLight` with a two-band normal-gated sky/ground fill plus a warm anti-sun bounce wrap, so shaded facades read cool and soffits read warm instead of everything being lifted by one grey constant; (2) make the sky HDR and make the IBL come from the SAME source as the visible sky, so the sun disc and horizon band actually carry energy into `scene.environment` instead of being clamped at 1.0; (3) add a per-arena declared photometric scale so `sunIntensity: 3.2` / `ambientIntensity: 0.42` stop being eyeballed per arena and the key:fill ratio becomes a gate-able number; (4) for Nuke Town specifically, the coarse interior-volume indirect gate — its houses are the one place we have interiors, and without a gate the inside of a house receives full skylight through a wall, which is why a doorway reads as a hole in a card rather than an aperture. Test1/Test2 are open-air and gain most from (1)+(2)+(3) plus the sky highlight roll-off and the analytic aureole, both of which are a handful of ALU and directly attack "our low-sun sky is a flat plateau". Everything here is presentation-only and touches no gameplay authority.

### One declared photometric scale, every light and the sky derived from it — adopt: **yes**

The engine picks a single conversion — 1 light-intensity unit = 25000 lux, 1 framebuffer radiance unit = 25000 cd/m² — writes it down once, and then DERIVES everything else instead of dialling it: extraterrestrial solar illuminance 128 klx becomes 5.12 units, noon sun after atmospheric extinction lands at ~3.9 (matching the pre-existing fallback sun of 4.3), clear zenith sky lands at ~0.06 radiance units, whole-sky diffuse comes out at ~15% of the beam. The file's header warns that a stray factor of π here is 1.65 stops of sky, and documents that the scattering integral already evaluates a radiance in the buffer's own convention (three's Lambert BRDF carries the 1/π), so it must NOT be multiplied by π on the way out. When that multiply was present, the sky was 1.65 stops hotter than the surfaces it lit — a sunlit wall darker than the sky behind it, clouds darker than the gaps between them. Every downstream constant (moonlight, cloud deck radiance, ground bounce) then divides by π to reach the same convention.

**Why:** Our per-arena numbers (`sunIntensity: 3.2` for Test1 and Nuke Town, `2.9` for Test2, `ambientIntensity: 0.42/0.46`) are absolute values with no stated reference, so 'is Test2 correctly two thirds of a stop under Test1?' is unanswerable and every arena drifts independently. A declared scale turns lighting parity into arithmetic a gate can check.

**In our engine:** Add `src/rendering/photometric-scale.ts` exporting `SCENE_LUX`, `sunIlluminanceForElevation(deg)`, and a `keyFillRatioStops(def)` helper. Extend the `lighting` block in `src/rendering/arena-visual-definition.ts` with an optional `photometry: { sceneLux, sunElevationDeg, expectedKeyFillStops }`, and add a validator alongside the existing practical checks (`arena-visual-definition.ts:308-337`) that fails if a definition's `sunIntensity / (ambientIntensity + skyFill)` falls outside 4-6 stops for an exterior arena. Backfill Test1 (hard mid-morning ≈ 45° sun), Test2 (golden hour ≈ 12°) and Nuke Town from the same function rather than by eye, and record the derived values in the arena docs so a later pass cannot silently re-eyeball them.

**Cost:** Half a day. Zero runtime cost — it is constants plus one test.

**Upstream evidence:** `src/sky/atmosphere.js:21-51`, `src/sky/atmosphere.js:54`, `src/sky/atmosphere.js:57`, `src/sky/atmosphere.js:261-266`, `src/render/index.js:249`

### Two-band normal-gated bounce fill injected into the material's indirect term (replacing flat ambient) — adopt: **yes**

There is no AmbientLight. Instead `MaterialPatcher` injects GLSL at `#include <lights_fragment_maps>` that adds two irradiance vectors to `irradiance` before the BRDF: `owSkyFill` (cool, upper hemisphere) gated by `smoothstep(-0.95, 0.85, N.y)` — a wide cosine-hemisphere gate, because a vertical wall genuinely sees half the sky — and `owGroundFill` (warm, sun-coloured through the ground albedo) gated by `smoothstep(-0.05, 0.7, -N.y)` so it can only land on soffits, undersides and low-angle faces. The two are GATED, not lerped: lerping put a warm street bounce on every wall and made shadows come out warmer than the sun that cast them. Both are occluded by `sqrt(AO)`, never AO — 'a fill term that AO can drive to zero is not a fill, it is another way to make a black hole'. On top of that sits a third term, `owSunBounce`, a tight wrapped-diffuse lobe from the ANTI-sun hemisphere (`dot(N, normalize(-sunDir.x, 0.28, -sunDir.z)) + 0.12) / 1.12`) that models the sunlit side of the street lighting the shaded side. The CPU side (`_updateBounceFill`) takes the hue from the sky's published `ambientColor`, normalises it, then pushes chroma out from its own luminance by k=1.18 — because the whole-sky average is much less blue than what an up-facing surface actually sees, and without the push a shaded facade measured B−R = +0.0002 (4.5% saturation, indistinguishable from grey). The cool band rides the sky's published irradiance rather than the key, so it survives at night when the key is a 0.05 moon. Budgets are deliberately tiny and are documented as the thing that sets the key:fill ratio: skyFill 0.32 of the sky reference, groundFill 0.013 of the key, bounceFill 0.008, and iblDiffuse cut to 0.030 to pay for it — at the old skyFill 0.09 / iblDiffuse 1.0 the indirect terms supplied 42% of every lit value and the key:fill ratio collapsed to 2.4:1 where real sun runs 5-8:1.

**Why:** This is the single biggest visual difference between upstream's frames and ours. A flat `AmbientLight` adds the same irradiance to a soffit, a north wall and a floor, which is precisely why our arenas read as 'lit by a constant'. Normal-gating costs three smoothsteps and buys shape in every shadowed area — the owner's 'lighting needs to be much better' is mostly this.

**In our engine:** Build `src/rendering/hemispheric-fill.ts` exporting a TSL node fragment: inputs `skyFillIrradiance` (vec3 uniform), `groundFillIrradiance` (vec3), `fillDirGates` (vec4 = -0.95, 0.85, -0.05, 0.7), `sunBounceWrap` (float) and `sunDirWorld`. On the WebGPU path, add it to the material's indirect diffuse via `NodeMaterial` (three r185 exposes per-material node overrides; the cheapest safe hook is a custom `MeshStandardNodeMaterial` subclass whose `.aoNode`/output we already control in `pass64-tsl-scene.ts`). Drive the uniforms from a new `ArenaVisualDefinition.lighting.fill` block: `{ skyFill, groundFill, bounceFill, skyHue, groundHue }`, computed once per arena switch from `sunColor`/`ambientColor` and the arena's ground albedo. Then DELETE the flat `AmbientLight` at `src/legacy-main.ts:3227` and reduce `ambientIntensity` to a small floor only. Because the WebGL2 compat path cannot take TSL, ship a WebGL2 fallback that keeps the existing `AmbientLight` plus a `HemisphereLight` matched to the two band colours — visually close enough, and the parity gate compares composition not exact pixels. Start at upstream's ratios (sky 0.32 of the sky reference, ground 0.013 of key, bounce 0.008) and tune against the existing `light-occlusion` review cameras in `test1.ts`, `test2.ts` and `atomic-acres.ts`, which are already positioned exactly where this term shows.

**Cost:** 2-3 days including the WebGL2 fallback. Runtime: 3 smoothsteps + 2 mad per fragment, well under 0.1 ms at 1080p. No new render targets.

**Upstream evidence:** `src/render/materialpatch.js:156-192`, `src/render/materialpatch.js:49-58`, `src/render/materialpatch.js:289-298`, `src/render/index.js:1091-1153`, `src/render/index.js:401-427`

### The IBL is baked from the SAME shader and the SAME uniform objects as the visible sky — adopt: **yes**

`SkyDome` builds two materials — `material` (screen, full-screen triangle, ray from `projectionMatrixInverse` so it picks up TAA jitter) and `envMaterial` (equirect) — that share ONE uniforms object and ONE `skSample()` body, differing only in a `quality` int that drops octaves and star points. `_bakeEnv()` blits `envMaterial` into a 512×256 HalfFloat equirect and runs `PMREMGenerator.fromEquirectangular` into a reused target, then publishes it via `render.setEnvMap`. The comment is explicit: sharing the uniform objects means 'the IBL can never disagree with' the visible sky. It only rebakes when the sun has moved more than 0.35° AND at most every 200 ms, so a frozen time of day pays nothing. Crucially the equirect is HalfFloat with `colorSpace = NoColorSpace` — the sun disc is authored at radiance 4000 (clamped down from the physical 75000 only because a half-float bloom target would overflow), so the disc, the aureole and the horizon band all carry real energy into the PMREM.

**Why:** Ours is structurally broken here and it is a citable defect, not a taste call: `generateArenaEnvironmentMap` sets `backgroundTexture.colorSpace = THREE.SRGBColorSpace` on an 8-bit canvas/WebP sky (`src/rendering/arena-environment-ibl.ts:72-73`), so the brightest thing our environment can contain is 1.0. Our painted sun disc (`sky-backdrop.ts:211-224`, `rgba(...,0.85)` core over a radial glow) is clamped to display white before it ever reaches PMREM. The result is an environment with no highlight, no specular event on metal, and a diffuse term that is essentially a blurred pastel — which is why our arenas' reflections look dead and the sun never glints off anything.

**In our engine:** Two steps. (a) Make the backdrop HDR: change `gradientTexture()` in `src/rendering/sky-backdrop.ts` to produce a `THREE.DataTexture` in `HalfFloatType` / `NoColorSpace` built by evaluating the existing gradient stops and `paintSun` in LINEAR light, with the sun core authored at 200-2000× the horizon band rather than at 0.85 alpha. Keep the 8-bit canvas as the WebGL2 fallback so `SKY_BACKDROP_TEXTURE_SIZE` and the existing `sky-backdrop.test.ts` contract still hold; add a test asserting the HDR variant's peak exceeds 50. (b) In `src/rendering/arena-environment-ibl.ts`, drop the `SRGBColorSpace` assignment for the HDR path and PMREM the HDR texture. The gate in `arena-environment-ibl.test.ts` should assert the PMREM peak is above 1.0. This is also the prerequisite for anything else on this list looking right — our bloom threshold is correctly >1 (`filmic-grade-chain.ts:580-583`) but nothing in the sky can currently exceed 1, so the sun physically cannot bloom.

**Cost:** 1-2 days. Runtime: identical (same PMREM, once per arena switch). Memory: 512×256 RGBA16F = 1 MB for the equirect.

**Upstream evidence:** `src/sky/dome.js:355-372`, `src/sky/index.js:836-848`, `src/sky/index.js:322-327`, `src/sky/index.js:492-493`, `src/sky/index.js:793`, `src/sky/fullscreen.js:76-100`

### Sky publishes a lighting contract; the renderer consumes it (ambientColor, indirectScale, exposureBias, cloudShadowAt) — adopt: **yes**

`SkySystem` exposes a small read-only surface the renderer and gameplay poll: `ambientColor` (a CPU stand-in for whole-sky irradiance so nothing needs a GPU readback), `indirectScale` (the indirect budget for the current sun elevation — ~0.45 at golden hour, 1.0 by day, 2.2 after dark), `exposureBias` (EV of metering compensation for this elevation) and `cloudShadowAt(x,z)`. The renderer multiplies its IBL diffuse budget by `indirectScale` and adds `exposureBias` to its own. The reasoning behind `indirectScale` is the interesting part: at a low sun the whole western sky lights up and a hemispherical PMREM average over-reports how much of that any VERTICAL surface can see, so a sunset ends up with shaded walls brighter than sunlit ones. Rather than fudging the sky, the indirect budget is brought DOWN on the same elevation curve the beam luminance is floored on, which makes the key:fill ratio elevation-invariant instead of only correct at 16:30. Symmetrically, `exposureBias` stops down 1.35 EV as the sun approaches the horizon (because a street canyon at 4° elevation is entirely in shadow and a geometry-weighted meter would open two stops and put the sky on the flat top of the curve) and half a stop after dark.

**Why:** Test2 is a golden-hour arena and this is exactly the failure it is exposed to: a low warm sun plus a bright PMREM'd horizon band gives shaded travertine that is brighter and less saturated than the sunlit travertine. `indirectScale` is a one-line fix for that class, and it generalises — Test1's high sun wants scale 1.0, Test2's low sun wants ~0.5, Nuke Town's sunset wants ~0.45.

**In our engine:** Add a derived-values block to `createProceduralArenaVisualDefinition` in `src/rendering/arena-visual-definition.ts`: from the authored sun elevation, compute `indirectScale` and `exposureBias` with upstream's curves (smoothstep over 0-14° for indirect, over 1-13° for exposure). Multiply `scene.environmentIntensity` by `indirectScale` where it is currently set (`arena-environment-ibl.ts`, the `budgetEnvironmentIntensity * arenaScale * reflectionScale` product) and feed `exposureBias` into the grade chain's exposure input in `src/rendering/filmic-grade-chain.ts`. Because our time of day is frozen per arena, these are constants baked at definition time — no per-frame work at all, and they become gate-able numbers in `arena-visual-definition.test.ts`.

**Cost:** Half a day. Zero runtime cost for us (frozen time of day makes them compile-time constants).

**Upstream evidence:** `src/sky/index.js:82-100`, `src/sky/index.js:733-772`, `src/render/index.js:1109-1153`, `src/render/index.js:1150-1152`

### Sky highlight roll-off: a power compressor on luminance only, keyed to the beam — adopt: **yes**

Before the sun/moon discs are added, the sky colour goes through `skRolloff`: if its Rec.709 luminance exceeds a knee, it is scaled by `pow(l/knee, p) * knee / l` with p = 0.34. Two deliberate choices. First it operates on LUMINANCE with chromaticity carried through unchanged, so the peach-to-crimson ramp of a sunset survives instead of desaturating to white the way a per-channel clamp does. Second it is a POWER compressor, not a Reinhard knee: Reinhard asymptotes at `knee*(1+room)`, a hard ceiling that piles everything from 8° off the sun out to the horizon onto one value and returns the sunset as a cream plateau — x^p has no ceiling, so a 40:1 overshoot still comes out as a 4:1 gradient. The knee is published as a FRACTION of the beam's own luminance (4.5% at a low sun rising to 11% by day) which makes it exposure-invariant, since autoexposure follows the beam too; a daylight zenith at 2% of the beam passes through untouched. The discs go in AFTER the roll-off because they are the one thing that is supposed to clip and bloom.

**Why:** Our sunset and golden-hour presets are authored as CSS colour stops that already sit near display white at the horizon (`sky-backdrop.ts:47-48`: `#fca94a`, `#ffd98a`), so once the HDR sky above lands, the low-sun band will be exactly the flat plateau upstream describes. A luminance-only power compressor is ~6 instructions and is the difference between a graded amber ramp and a cream void.

**In our engine:** Once the HDR backdrop from the IBL technique exists, apply the same compressor while BAKING the sky texture in `src/rendering/sky-backdrop.ts` rather than per-fragment — our sky is a static texture, so this becomes zero runtime cost. Add `rollOffKnee` and `rollOffPower` to each entry in `SKY_BACKDROP_GRADIENTS` (or derive the knee as a fraction of the arena's `sunIntensity`), and keep the sun disc OUT of the compressor so it stays the only thing above the bloom threshold. Add a test in `sky-backdrop.test.ts` asserting that for `sunset-farmland` and `estate-golden-hour` the horizon band's post-roll-off luminance spread across the bottom 20% of the texture exceeds some minimum, i.e. it is a gradient and not a plateau.

**Cost:** Half a day. Zero runtime cost (bake-time).

**Upstream evidence:** `src/sky/dome.js:120-155`, `src/sky/dome.js:265-270`, `src/sky/index.js:729-740`

### Analytic circumsolar aureole that restores the Mie forward peak — adopt: **partial**

A LUT-based sky at 384×192 has ~1° per texel of azimuth, and the Mie phase at g=0.8 puts most of its energy inside 5°, so bilinear interpolation destroys exactly that peak — which is why a Hillaire sky renders the sun as a hard white dot pasted on flat blue. `skAureole` adds it back analytically inside a 24° cone: aerosol optical depth along the ray (`σ_mie · uMieScale · 0.0012 / max(0.055, rayDir.y + 0.055)`) times the EXCESS of the Mie phase over its value at the cutoff, so the term is continuous at the cone edge, vanishes as turbidity goes to zero, and reddens with the sun because it is multiplied by the same `skTransmittance` as everything else. The one hand-picked coefficient (4.2) is explicitly documented as correcting a sampling error rather than a physical quantity, and is set so the aureole is about a stop over the surrounding sky at 8° out. The same function is called for the moon, so the lunar halo is the same fraction of the moonlit sky for free.

**Why:** The physics motivation (LUT undersampling) does not apply to us — we have no LUT. But the VISUAL is the thing our painted `paintSun` radial glow is trying and failing to be: our glow is a fixed-radius alpha blob in sRGB, so it neither reddens with the sun colour nor carries any energy. Rebuilding it as `irradiance · transmittance · (miePhaseExcess · mieOpticalDepth)` gives a halo whose hue and reach follow the arena's `sunColor` and its atmosphere `dust`/`mist` for free.

**In our engine:** Replace the `glow` radial gradient in `paintSun` (`src/rendering/sky-backdrop.ts:211-230`) with an evaluated Cornette-Shanks excess term over the angular distance from the sun centre, scaled by the preset's `dust` value from the arena's `atmosphere` block, and tinted by the arena's `sunColor`. Author it in the HDR bake so it lands around 1 stop over the local sky at 8° out, as upstream does. This makes the aureole automatically consistent across Test1 (dust 0.22, small tight halo), Test2 (dust 0.08 but a low sun, so a long path and a wide amber halo) and Nuke Town (dust 0.28, sunset). Keep the sun CORE out of it — the core is the bloom source.

**Cost:** Half a day, folded into the HDR sky bake. Zero runtime cost.

**Upstream evidence:** `src/sky/dome.js:85-115`, `src/sky/dome.js:207-210`, `src/sky/luts.js:243-247`

### Real punctual practicals, with zero-intensity ballast lights holding the shader-permutation count constant — adopt: **yes**

The world owns 17 real `PointLight` practicals — 12 interior bulbs at 13 m range and 5 street lamps at 22 m. The renderer distance-culls them (`fade = 1 - smoothstep(d, 0.75r, 1.15r)`, `light.visible = fade > 0.002`), and three bakes the number of VISIBLE point lights into every material's program cache key, so walking down the street swept the count through 9-8-7-6-5-4 and recompiled every lit material at each step. Measured: '186 programs and ~3.5 s of stalls inside 900 frames of play', with individual hitches of 636-702 ms. The fix is not to remove the lights: 24 extra `PointLight(0x000000, 0, 0.01)` are parked 1000 m under the map, NOT registered for culling, and `_stabiliseLightCount` tops the visible count up to a fixed 20 every `lateUpdate`. A light whose colour × intensity is exactly zero adds a float 0.0 to the irradiance accumulator — 'this cannot move a pixel no matter how many slots are lit', it only changes `numPointLights`. Measured cost of the padding: p05 frame time 15.7 → 14.4 ms, i.e. inside noise. The count is PREDICTED with the renderer's own cull formula rather than read from last frame's `visible` flags, because the renderer culls after `lateUpdate` and the off-by-one frames are exactly the frames that used to stall. **Why:** Our 'interior point lights are emissive-only' rule (`test1.ts:24-26`, `test2.ts:22-24`, `atomic-acres.ts:17-19`) exists for exactly the reason upstream measured — pipeline-variant churn — and upstream shows the constraint is solvable rather than fundamental. An emissive-only lamp casts no light on the wall behind it, which is why our interiors and our night-adjacent arenas have no light pools. For Nuke Town in particular, the two houses are the arena's whole interior story and right now nothing inside them is actually lit by a lamp.

On WebGPU/TSL the churn mechanism is different in detail but identical in kind — the node graph's light count is still a pipeline key — so measure before and after with the existing `render-runtime-device-features` instrumentation rather than assuming. Ship it behind the existing per-arena definition so Test1/Test2 (genuinely open-air) can stay `emissive-only` and Nuke Town's houses get real bulbs. **Cost:** 3-4 days including measurement on both backends. Runtime cost of the ballast itself measured as inside noise upstream; verify independently on D3D12.

### Practicals held against the key by a global trim, and gated on solar altitude — adopt: **yes**

Two mechanisms. First, `_cullLights` applies `settings.practicalGain` (0.55, half a stop) to every light registered with a range at or under `PRACTICAL_RANGE = 30` — room and street lights — while deliberately NOT touching the FX flash pool, which registers at 90 m precisely so the trim cannot bite a muzzle flash. The rationale is that the practicals set two of the renderer's headline ratios (interior-to-exterior through a doorway, and warm-pool-to-cool-ambient at night), and at unity an interior metered within 1.3 stops of the sunlit facade framed in its own doorway where a real one runs 4-5. Second, the world drives intensity off the sky's real `sunAltitude` rather than a timer: street lamps ramp `14 * mix` where `mix = 1 - clamp((alt + 0.05)/0.16)`, and interior bulbs run `5 + 17*mix` — 'a 60 W bulb is NOT competitive with daylight', and running it at night strength at noon made every interior read as pure tungsten and sit level with the sunlit street.

**Why:** Independent of whether we adopt real punctual practicals, the ratio discipline applies to our EMISSIVE practicals right now: our emissive fixtures are authored at whatever `emissiveIntensity` looked right in isolation, and there is no arena-level control that says 'a lamp is 1.5 stops under the sunlit wall'. Nuke Town's sunset arena is the one where a too-hot emissive fixture will read as a bug.

### Coarse interior-volume gate on all indirect light — adopt: **partial**

Skylight cannot reach the middle of a closed room, and letting it do so makes a doorway read as a hole cut in a card rather than an aperture two stops over the room it lights. Rather than per-room geometry, the renderer publishes up to 10 coarse boxes — the enterable buildings' own footprints, already available as `world.buildings[].spec` — into uniforms, in LEVEL space (the level is authored on one yaw, so world→level is a 2D rotate cheap enough per fragment). `owInteriorGate` tests DEPTH INSIDE the box rather than containment, because a facade's outer skin sits exactly on the footprint boundary at depth 0 and its inner skin one wall thickness in, so a 6-30 cm smoothstep feather separates the two faces of the same wall with no per-room data. Buildings marked `collapse` or `ruin` are excluded — 'a shell open to the sky must keep its skylight, or the one room with a hole in its roof is the one that reads as a cave'. An AO-derived gate is mixed in at 0.6 so untagged pockets (arcades, stairwells, awnings) also lose skylight. The gate multiplies `iblIrradiance`, the sky fill band, the ground band AND the sun-bounce wrap; without it on the wrap, 'the inside of every room was receiving the street's bounce at full strength through a metre of masonry'. It bottoms out at `interiorIndirect = 0.035`, a floor, never zero. Runs once when the world first appears.

**Why:** Directly applicable to Nuke Town, which is the only one of the three arenas with interiors, and whose review cameras already include `nuke-town-aqua-wall-closed` and `nuke-town-aqua-door-open` — i.e. we already have the exact probes this technique is designed to fix. Not applicable to Test1 or Test2, both of which are explicitly open-air, so it should be optional per arena rather than global.

**In our engine:** Add an optional `interiorVolumes: readonly { center: [x,z], half: [hx,hz], y: [y0,y1] }[]` (max 8) to `ArenaVisualDefinition`, populated for `atomic-acres` from the house footprints the arena builder already knows. Implement `src/rendering/interior-indirect-gate.ts` as a TSL node that does the depth-inside-box test and returns a scalar, and multiply it into the environment/IBL diffuse contribution and into the new two-band fill. Since our arenas are not rotated, we can skip the level-space transform entirely — test in world space. Reuse the existing `light-occlusion` and `portal` review-camera classes as the gate: the closed-wall probe must measure materially darker than the open-door probe, which is a number `arena-visual-definition.test.ts` can assert against a captured HDR sample. Keep the floor (0.035) so nothing goes black — a black interior is a gameplay regression, not a look.

**Cost:** 2 days for Nuke Town only. Runtime: one loop over ≤8 boxes per fragment, ~30 ALU; negligible, but it should be compiled out for arenas with no volumes.

### AO on indirect only, with a small deliberate fraction on direct as micro-shadow — adopt: **yes**

The AO buffer is not multiplied over the frame. Inside `lights_fragment_maps` it multiplies `irradiance` and `iblIrradiance` through Jimenez's GTAO multi-bounce polynomial (so dark albedos occlude more than bright ones, which stops AO turning white plaster into grey mud) and applies a roughness-widened specular occlusion `pow(ao, 1 + r²·2)` to the IBL specular. Direct sunlight is left alone — 'AO darkens bounce light and leaves direct sunlight alone', which is the reason it reads as occlusion rather than a dirty grey overlay. But a small fraction (0.35 of the AO strength) IS applied to the direct term as micro-shadowing, with a stated reason: a cascade texel is tens of centimetres wide and the contact ray only runs along the sun direction, so the last centimetre of a wall/soffit junction gets occlusion from neither and the frame comes back with razor-sharp junctions and nothing grounded. The AO visibility is also floored at 0.25 before use, because 'an AO term that reaches 0 is a dark halo, not occlusion'.

**Why:** Whatever occlusion we run (we have `light-occlusion.ts` and a contact-occlusion stage in the grade chain, `filmic-grade-chain.ts:11`), applying it as a post-multiply over the composited frame is the classic mistake and produces exactly the 'grey wash in the corners' look. Moving it to indirect-only, plus the multi-bounce polynomial and the 0.25 floor, costs a handful of instructions and is a large quality step.

**In our engine:** Audit where `src/rendering/light-occlusion.ts` and the 'contact occlusion' stage in `LINEAR_SOURCE_STAGES` actually apply their term. If it is a full-frame multiply after the scene pass, move it into the material's indirect path alongside the new two-band fill in `hemispheric-fill.ts` — one TSL node that takes the AO sample and returns `owMultiBounce(ao, albedo)` for diffuse and `pow(ao, 1+r²·2)` for specular, with the 0.25 floor. Keep a small direct-light micro-shadow term at 0.35 strength. Note this changes composited pixels, so it needs a fresh benchmark capture under `game-release-benchmark-guard` rather than being slipped in.

**Cost:** 1 day of implementation, plus a benchmark-guard cycle because it moves pixels everywhere.

**Upstream evidence:** `src/render/materialpatch.js:131-145`, `src/render/materialpatch.js:116-124`, `src/render/materialpatch.js:281-313`, `src/render/materialpatch.js:4-17`

### CSM done as bounding-sphere fit + texel snap + PCSS, with per-cascade cylinder caster culling — adopt: **partial**

N cascades in one R32F `sampler2DArray` (one texture unit total, so materials keep their own map slots). Splits use a lambda=0.86 mix of logarithmic and uniform. Each cascade is fitted to the BOUNDING SPHERE of its sub-frustum so the ortho extent is rotation-invariant, the radius is quantised (`ceil(r*16)/16`) against float drift, and the projection origin is then snapped to whole texels — together this removes shadow swimming completely because the sampled texel grid is nailed to world space rather than the camera. Filtering is PCSS: a Vogel-disk blocker search, a penumbra estimate `gap · tan(sunAngularRadius)`, then a Vogel PCF at the derived radius, giving contact-hardening. Bias is normal-offset (`texelWorld · (0.55 + 1.1·(1-NdL))`) plus slope-scaled depth bias, both expressed in world units derived from the cascade's own texel size. The last 12% of each cascade cross-fades into the next and the far edge fades to unshadowed so there is no terminator. The map size is deliberately capped at 2048 with the note that '4 × 4096 × R32F is a quarter of a gigabyte for shadows nobody can see' and '2048 with PCSS reads sharper than 4096 without it'. The caster cull is the budget win: rather than three's frustum test against the cascade's ortho BOX, it tests against the extruded CYLINDER (the shader only ever samples a cascade for receivers inside its fit sphere, so the box corners — 1−π/4 of the cross-section — are sampled by nothing), with a 32-texel margin sized to cover the texel snap, the normal offset, the 10-texel blocker search and the 9-texel PCF disc. That margin was validated: at 2 texels the pass was measurably NOT pixel-neutral (0.04% of pixels, up to 26/255).

**Why:** Replacing three r185's WebGPU shadow path wholesale is a large, high-risk change against our strict parity gates, and TSL shadow sampling would have to be hand-written per material. But four sub-parts are individually adoptable and cheap: the texel-snap stabilisation, the 0.86 split lambda, the cascade cross-fade, and the caster cull. The map-size finding is also directly relevant — we author 2048 everywhere (`test1.ts:27`, `test2.ts:27`, `atomic-acres.ts:23`), which matches upstream's conclusion, so that is confirmation rather than a change.

**In our engine:** Do NOT rewrite the shadow pipeline. Instead: (a) if we currently drive a single directional shadow camera, add cascade splits with lambda 0.86 and a texel snap on the shadow camera's projection offset — this alone kills swimming when the player moves and is maybe 20 lines in whatever configures `sunLight.shadow`; (b) implement the cylinder caster cull as a pre-shadow visibility pass in `src/rendering/runtime-shadow-budget.ts` (which today is 12 lines and only handles the viewmodel scope), extended to hide static arena meshes that cannot reach the shadow frustum, with upstream's 32-texel margin and a pixel-neutrality test comparing a captured frame with and without the cull; (c) keep 2048 and cite upstream's measurement in the arena docs so nobody 'improves' it to 4096. Nuke Town's `maximumDistance: 176` over 1.6 M triangles is where the caster cull pays.

**Cost:** Texel snap + splits: 1 day. Caster cull: 2 days plus a pixel-neutrality gate. Runtime: the cull is net-negative cost (fewer draws).

**Upstream evidence:** `src/render/csm.js:33-46`, `src/render/csm.js:128-200`, `src/render/csm.js:227-310`, `src/render/csm.js:460-560`, `src/core/config.js:52-81`

### Bloom as an energy-preserving dual-filter pyramid with an exposure-scaled soft-knee prefilter, added not mixed — adopt: **partial**

Jimenez's COD:AW progressive dual-filter pyramid. The 13-tap downsample applies a Karis luminance average on the FIRST level only, so one hot pixel cannot pump a whole mip. The soft-knee prefilter also runs on the first level only, and it runs AFTER multiplying by the exposure texture — 'a fixed linear threshold on unscaled radiance would mean something different at every time of day'. It is max-channel driven so a saturated red tracer blooms as readily as a white highlight. Upsampling is a 9-tap tent blended back up the chain at alpha 0.5 with normal blending, i.e. `lerp(dst, src, 0.5)`, which is energy PRESERVING — 'adding the mips outright (what most WebGL bloom does) multiplies total energy by the mip count and turns the whole frame into haze'. The composite ADDS the pyramid rather than mixing it, because the pyramid now only carries what is above display white. The two widest mips get their tent radius cut to 0.62 and their blend weight to 0.34, with a stated reason: a radius-1 tent on a 1/64-res mip is a 30-pixel halo and that halo is what dissolves a roofline against a bright sky. The threshold is 1.6, raised from 0.85 because 'a daylight sky lands around 1.0-1.5 in exposure-scaled linear light', so at 0.85 the SKY was the brightest thing in the pyramid and 'an enemy on a balcony measured 3% contrast against the cloud he was standing in front of'.

**Why:** We already have a depth-guarded bloom with a hard fail if the threshold is not above 1.0 (`filmic-grade-chain.ts:575-587`), and that fail-closed rule is arguably better than upstream's — it is a combat-safety rule, and upstream's own 3%-contrast anecdote is exactly the outcome our rule prevents. What we can still take are three specific things: the Karis first-mip average, the reduced radius/weight on the top two mips, and the confirmation that a daylight sky in exposure-scaled linear lands near 1.0-1.5 (which sets where our threshold must sit once the sky becomes HDR).

**In our engine:** In `src/rendering/filmic-grade-chain.ts`, extend `TunableBloomNode`/`tuneBloomForProfile` with a per-mip radius/weight schedule and set the widest two levels to radius 0.62, weight 0.34. Confirm three's TSL bloom applies a Karis average on the first downsample; if it does not, that is the one place a custom node is worth writing. Most importantly: once the HDR sky lands, re-derive `profile.bloom.threshold` per grade profile against a measured exposure-scaled sky luminance for each arena rather than leaving it at whatever satisfies `> 1`. Nuke Town's sunset sky is the arena where a threshold set for a daylight sky will bloom the whole upper half of the frame.

**Cost:** 1 day. Runtime unchanged (same pyramid, different constants).

**Upstream evidence:** `src/render/bloom.js:15-21`, `src/render/bloom.js:33-43`, `src/render/bloom.js:107-127`, `src/render/bloom.js:176-198`, `src/render/index.js:344-358`

### Order of operations across the display transform: lens effects in linear, grade in display — adopt: **partial**

Chromatic aberration, bloom and the cos⁴ lens vignette all happen BEFORE the tone map, because they are linear-light lens phenomena. The vignette comment is the clearest evidence of why: applied in display space at 0.24 it was a flat multiply on the code value that scaled everything outside the middle sixth of the frame by 0.85-0.81, 'which put a hard ceiling of ~210 code values on the sky and made display white unreachable anywhere but dead centre'; in linear light the same 0.24 costs a quarter of a stop that the filmic shoulder absorbs in the highlights while still weighting the mids and shadows. Conversely the grade LUT, the grain and the dither happen AFTER the sRGB encode, because the LUT is authored display-referred with additive toe and split-tone offsets in code values — 'feeding it linear light turned a 0.008 toe into a hard linear floor and painted the whole frame's shadows blue-grey'. The tone map itself is AgX with slope/power/saturation applied in LOG space, chosen over ACES specifically for the absence of the magenta-highlight problem. Grain response is deliberately inverted from the naive version: LESS grain in the darks, because there it reads as a dirty image.

**Why:** We already separate `LINEAR_SOURCE_STAGES` from `FILMIC_GRADE_CHAIN_STAGES` (`filmic-grade-chain.ts:91-116`) and have display-referred toe and split-tone stages, so the architecture is right. The concrete things worth checking against upstream are the vignette's side of the transform and the grain's response curve — both are one-line placements that produce large, obvious artefacts if they land on the wrong side.

**In our engine:** Audit `FILMIC_GRADE_CHAIN_STAGES` and `buildFilmicGradeChain` for where a vignette (if any) is applied; if it is display-referred, move it to `LINEAR_SOURCE_STAGES` as a cos⁴ falloff. Confirm `grainAmplitudeFor` weights toward the mids rather than the shadows (`filmic-grade-chain.ts:191-206`); if it is uniform or darker-weighted, apply upstream's `0.35 + 0.65·smoothstep(0, 0.30, luma)` response. Both are covered by existing grade-chain tests, so they are low-risk edits with an immediate regression signal.

**Cost:** Half a day of audit; edits are constants.

**Upstream evidence:** `src/render/composite.js:5-13`, `src/render/composite.js:118-133`, `src/render/composite.js:134-158`, `src/render/glsl.js:130-160`, `src/render/index.js:88-95`

### Viewmodel lit by its own view-space three-point rig in its own scene, keyed off the scene's light level — adopt: **yes**

The weapon lives in `viewScene` and gets a five-light rig — key, cool fill, warm rim, warm bounce-from-below, and a hemisphere — with every direction authored in VIEW space and rotated into the scene each frame, so the weapon's key/fill/rim separation is invariant to the world sun's azimuth. The reason is stated plainly: handing the gun a copy of the world sun means that whenever the sun is behind the gun the camera-facing side gets nothing, and weapon albedos are physically correct (anodised aluminium is 0.026 linear), so it goes to a black silhouette. The key intensity is a RATIO of the scene's own reference level (`max(sunIntensity, ambientLevel/0.15)`) shaped by `pow(min(ref/REF_DAYLIGHT,1), 0.65)` — sub-linear so a night scene, where the meter is exposure-locked, does not put the weapon back in silhouette; the gamma is a no-op in full daylight. Fill takes the cool sky hue, rim takes the warm key hue, and the below-bounce takes the world's own ground-bounce hue so the glove picks up the same colour the buildings do. The rig is 0.34 of the key, sized to lift the support glove out of the handguard's own cast shadow without competing. The whole viewmodel is also resolved into its own MSAA colour+depth target and composited after TAA, because view-space motion produces zero velocity and TAA was blending 85% stale background into the optic tube.

**Why:** We already have a viewmodel shadow scope (`runtime-shadow-budget.ts:3-7`), so the viewmodel is a first-class concept for us. A weapon lit by the arena's single directional sun will be a black silhouette on Test2 whenever the player faces away from the golden-hour key, and dead flat on Nuke Town's sunset — this is the cheapest large win on the list, because it is five lights on a scene containing one object.

**In our engine:** Add `src/rendering/viewmodel-light-rig.ts` creating key/fill/rim/bounce directional lights plus a hemisphere in the viewmodel scene, with upstream's view-space directions (key upper-front-left `(-0.45, 0.75, 0.55)`, fill lower-front-right `(0.6, -0.15, 0.5)`, rim from behind `(0.2, 0.35, -0.9)`, bounce from below-front `(-0.2, -0.86, 0.47)`), re-pointed each frame from the view camera's world matrix. Drive the key as a ratio of the arena's `sunIntensity` with the 0.65 gamma, take the fill hue from `ambientColor` and the rim/bounce hues from `sunColor`, both already in every `ArenaVisualDefinition`. Add the ratios (`viewKeyScale 0.55`, `viewFillRatio 0.3`, `viewRimRatio 0.5`, `viewHemiRatio 0.16`, `viewBounceRatio 0.34`) as shared constants, not per-arena, so the weapon reads identically across arenas — which is the point. Ensure the rig lights are tagged so `runtime-shadow-budget.ts` keeps them out of the arena shadow budget.

**Cost:** 1 day. Runtime: five lights on a scene with one mesh; negligible.

### Analytic aerial perspective as the always-on floor, marched volumetrics as the optional tier — adopt: **partial**

The volumetric system splits into a half-res march (24-56 exponentially distributed steps, dithered start, dual-lobe HG phase, shadowed by the cascade array and by the cumulus deck), a temporal resolve with velocity reprojection and a 3×3 neighbourhood clamp, and a full-res composite. When `q.volumetrics` is off, steps 1 and 2 are skipped and the composite falls back to a fully analytic single-scattering approximation — 'no shafts, but the aerial perspective is identical, so a low-end machine still gets the correct distance falloff rather than a different-looking scene'. The distance term itself is the closed form of the exponential-height integral, so haze on geometry is crisp and noise-free at full resolution instead of a banded low-res alpha. Scattering and extinction are deliberately NOT coupled by an albedo: extinction is set by the visibility wanted down the street, the inscatter gain by how readable the shafts need to be, because 'a physically closed system either has invisible shafts outdoors or milk at 200 m'. The haze COLOUR is angle-dependent, mixing the cosine-weighted whole-sky average against the horizon-band average (the two texels of the tiny 2×1 ambient LUT) with the key's own transmitted spectrum folded into the forward half — so distance separates by HUE, not by grey. The shaft gain is applied to the anisotropic EXCESS of the phase function only, written as `p + max(0, p−iso)·(g−1)`, because scaling the whole phase also scales its 1/4π floor and 'that floor is not a shaft, it is a veil over every pixel'. Near-field scattering is ramped in over the first 12 m so the fog tuned for a 200 m street does not lay a wash over the weapon and hands.

**Why:** The marched shafts are a real cost (~1.5 ms of a 10-12 ms frame at 56 steps half-res) and a large TSL surface with a WebGL2 parity problem, so they belong at most behind an ultra tier. But two ideas transfer at essentially zero cost and directly fix our fog: our fog is a single `color` constant per arena (`test1.ts:26` `0xd9cdb2`, `test2.ts:26` `0xe4cfae`, `atomic-acres.ts:22` `0xb1c0be`), which is exactly the 'one number cannot be both the amber you get looking into a low sun and the blue you get looking away' failure — and it is worst on Test2 and Nuke Town, our two low-sun arenas.

**In our engine:** Split our per-arena `fog` from one colour into two — a cool whole-sky value and a warm horizon-band value — and blend them per fragment by `f = (0.5 + 0.5·dot(viewDir, sunDir))²`, with the warm end additionally tinted toward the arena's `sunColor`. That is one dot product and a lerp in whatever fog node the TSL scene builds, and it turns distance into a colour-temperature cue instead of a grey wash. Also add the 12 m near-field ramp, which will remove the haze wash currently sitting on the weapon and near geometry. Derive both fog colours from the sky backdrop at bake time (sample the HDR equirect's cosine-weighted upper hemisphere and its horizon band — upstream computes exactly this in a 2×1 target, `src/sky/luts.js:208-236`) so they cannot disagree with the sky. Keep the marched shafts OUT of scope for now; revisit only if an ultra tier is created.

**Cost:** 1-2 days for the two-colour fog and near-field ramp. Runtime: a dot product and a lerp. The marched shafts, if ever taken, are a separate multi-week project.

**Upstream evidence:** `src/sky/volumetrics.js:7-35`, `src/sky/volumetrics.js:56-77`, `src/sky/volumetrics.js:84-105`, `src/sky/volumetrics.js:107-118`, `src/sky/index.js:114-122`, `src/sky/index.js:330-336`

The correct extraction is the SHAPE of the result, not the model: a baked HDR equirect with a real sun disc, a roll-off, an aureole and a two-texel ambient probe gets us most of the frame for a fraction of the surface area.

- NOT adopted: SUN_KEY_GAIN = 1.55 (src/sky/index.js:26-45). Copying the fudge without copying the debt it services would just hide a materials problem behind a lighting knob, and our streamline cadence is explicitly against that. If our sunlit surfaces come out under-bright after the photometric scale lands, fix the albedos.

- NOT adopted: Full adaptive auto-exposure with the metering chain (src/render/exposure.js). The GPU log-luminance reduction with no readback is genuinely good engineering, and the depth-plus-luminance sky de-weighting is a clever fix for 'the sky pulls the street to night'. But an exposure that opens up when a player walks into a shadowed room and closes when they step out is a VISIBILITY change in a competitive shooter — two players looking at the same corridor from different rooms would see different contrast on a target, and it would make our HDR review-camera captures non-deterministic, breaking the benchmark-guard comparisons the release process depends on. Take the fixed per-arena exposure bias derived from sun elevation (technique 4) and leave the adaptation out. If auto-exposure is ever wanted, it must be clamped to well under a stop of travel and gated by the benchmark guard.

- NOT adopted: Replacing three's shadow pipeline with the in-material PCSS CSM (src/render/csm.js:460-560 plus the materialpatch injection at src/render/materialpatch.js:111-129). Upstream can do this because it patches GLSL into three's WebGL forward path via onBeforeCompile with one shared uniform object. On r185 WebGPURenderer/TSL there is no equivalent global injection point — we would be hand-authoring shadow sampling into every material's node graph and permanently forking away from three's shadow updates, against strict parity gates. The stabilisation sub-parts (texel snap, split lambda, cross-fade, caster cull) are worth taking on their own; the PCSS-in-material architecture is not.

- NOT adopted: Screen-space reflections, motion blur, ADS depth of field, chromatic aberration and film grain (src/render/ssr.js, motionblur.js, dof.js, and the CA/grain sections of composite.js). Elegant — the sun dims by up to a stop as a cloud crosses it, and the CPU and GPU agree because both evaluate the same four analytic waves.
